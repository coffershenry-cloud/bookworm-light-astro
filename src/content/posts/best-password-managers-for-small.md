<div style="color: #263238; font-family: Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif; font-size: 16px; line-height: 1.75;">

<p>Small businesses rely on dozens of online accounts to operate. Employees may need access to email, banking dashboards, accounting software, customer databases, social media accounts, cloud storage and project management tools.</p><p><img alt="Page 2 | Password manager Images - Free Download on Magnific (formerly  Freepik)" class="sFlh5c Jm0Uqe FyHeAf uT7N3d" height="448" src="https://img.magnific.com/free-photo/securing-digital-data-laptop-cyber-security-padlock_23-2151998496.jpg?semt=ais_hybrid&amp;w=740&amp;q=80" style="--xgflle: 1.4285714285714286; max-width: 740px;" width="640" />&nbsp;</p>

<p>Managing all those passwords can quickly become a security problem. Employees may reuse passwords, save credentials in unsecured documents or share logins through email and messaging apps. If one account is compromised, attackers may gain access to other business systems.</p>

<p>A business password manager helps solve this problem by providing a secure place to store credentials, generate strong passwords and share access with authorized team members. More advanced plans also provide administrative controls, security reports and tools for managing employee access.</p>

<p>But which password manager should a small business choose?</p>

<p>In this guide, we compare five business password managers: 1Password, Bitwarden, Keeper, Dashlane and LastPass. We examine their business features, pricing, strengths, limitations and the types of organizations they suit best.</p>

<div style="background: rgb(242, 239, 255); border-left: 5px solid rgb(117, 70, 200); border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0px; padding: 18px 20px;">
<p style="color: #49318a; font-weight: bold; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">Quick answer: Which business password manager is best?</p>
<p style="margin: 0px;">Bitwarden is a strong value option for small businesses that need centralized credential management without a high per-user subscription. 1Password is a compelling choice for teams that prioritize usability and administration. Keeper offers a low-cost entry plan for very small teams, Dashlane adds credential-risk monitoring options, and LastPass provides a familiar business password-management environment with several administration tiers.</p>
</div>

<p><b>Who this guide is for:</b> Small and growing U.S. businesses that want to manage employee passwords, shared credentials and access to business accounts. Prices are in U.S. dollars unless stated otherwise.</p>

<h2 style="border-bottom: 2px solid rgb(228, 216, 245); color: #45247a; padding-bottom: 9px;">1. Bitwarden — Best Value for Small Businesses</h2>

<p><b>Bitwarden</b> is an open-source password manager that offers personal and business plans. Its business products provide centralized management and secure credential sharing, making it an attractive option for small organizations that want useful administrative features without paying for a more expensive enterprise-oriented package.</p><p><img alt="Bitwarden Password Manager - GCA Cybersecurity Toolkit | Tools and  Resources to Improve Your Cyber Defenses" class="sFlh5c Jm0Uqe FyHeAf uT7N3d" height="386" src="https://encrypted-tbn0.gstatic.com/images?q=tbn:ANd9GcRtwwzbmU6A6BuqhzbX9qWBYEOej5FIgpNS6rPchU2spWNjmqpWzYg4JR8&amp;s=10" style="--xgflle: 1.658139534883721; max-width: 2560px;" width="640" />&nbsp;</p>

<p>One of Bitwarden's main advantages is its combination of relatively affordable pricing, an open-source development model and business-focused access management.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">Key Bitwarden business features</h3>

<ul>
<li><b>Secure credential storage:</b> Store employee passwords and other sensitive login information in encrypted vaults.</li>
<li><b>Organization sharing:</b> Share credentials with authorized colleagues through organizational collections.</li>
<li><b>Centralized administration:</b> Manage organization membership and shared access from a central account.</li>
<li><b>Event logs:</b> Review activity associated with organizational credential management.</li>
<li><b>Directory synchronization:</b> Connect supported identity directories to help manage employee access.</li>
<li><b>SCIM provisioning:</b> Automate aspects of user provisioning and deprovisioning on eligible plans.</li>
<li><b>Open-source foundation:</b> Bitwarden makes its source code available for inspection, contributing to transparency around its software.</li>
<li><b>Self-hosting option:</b> Organizations with the appropriate Enterprise arrangement can explore hosting their own Bitwarden server.</li>
</ul>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">Bitwarden pricing</h3>

<p>Bitwarden's Teams plan costs <b>$4 per user per month</b> when billed annually. Its Enterprise plan costs <b>$6 per user per month</b> with annual billing.</p>

<h2 style="border-bottom: 2px solid rgb(228, 216, 245); color: #45247a; padding-bottom: 9px;">What Should a Small Business Look for in a Password Manager?</h2>

<p>Comparing password managers by price alone can lead to a poor decision. A business needs to consider how credentials are stored, shared, monitored and managed when employees join or leave.</p>

<p>Here are the most important features to evaluate.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">1. Secure password storage</h3>

<p>A password manager should protect stored credentials using strong encryption. Look for a provider that clearly explains its encryption architecture, how keys are managed and what information the service provider can access.</p>

<p>Many established password managers use a zero-knowledge design. In simple terms, this means the provider is designed so it cannot read the contents of users' encrypted vaults. However, the exact implementation differs between providers, so review the security documentation rather than relying on the phrase alone.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">2. Password generation</h3>

<p>Employees should not have to invent a different password for every business account. A password manager should generate long, random and unique credentials.</p>

<p>This reduces the temptation to reuse a familiar password across multiple services.</p>

<p>CISA recommends long, random and unique passwords, and advises businesses to use password managers to help employees manage them.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">3. Secure sharing and team vaults</h3>

<p>Employees sometimes need access to the same business accounts. For example, a marketing team may share access to a social media account, or several authorized employees may need credentials for a supplier portal.</p>

<p>A business password manager should allow administrators to share credentials without exposing passwords through ordinary email or chat messages.</p>

<p>Look for shared vaults or folders, permission settings and the ability to remove access when responsibilities change.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">4. Administrative controls</h3>

<p>Business password management is different from personal password storage. Administrators need visibility into who has access to company credentials and the ability to manage that access.</p>

<p>Useful controls may include:</p>

<ul>
<li>Adding and removing employees.</li>
<li>Assigning roles and permissions.</li>
<li>Creating shared vaults and collections.</li>
<li>Enforcing security policies.</li>
<li>Reviewing account activity.</li>
<li>Removing access when an employee leaves.</li>
</ul>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">5. Multifactor authentication</h3>

<p>Multifactor authentication (MFA) adds another verification step when someone signs in. This can make it harder for an attacker to access an account using only a stolen password.</p>

<p>Look for MFA options that suit your organization, such as authenticator apps, security keys or other supported authentication methods.</p>

<p>Remember that having an authenticator feature inside a password manager does not mean every business account is automatically protected by MFA. You must configure MFA for the relevant accounts and services.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">6. Employee onboarding and offboarding</h3>

<p>When a new employee joins, administrators should be able to provide access to the accounts needed for their role.</p>

<p>When an employee leaves, access should be removed promptly. Shared passwords may also need to be rotated, particularly if the former employee knew credentials that were not individually assigned.</p>

<p>Directory synchronization and automated provisioning can make this process easier for larger teams, although some of these features are restricted to higher-tier plans.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">7. Security reporting and alerts</h3>

<p>Some business password managers provide dashboards and reports that help administrators identify weak, reused or potentially exposed credentials.</p>

<p>These features can help a business prioritize password improvements. However, alerts are only useful when someone reviews them and takes appropriate action.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">8. Recovery and account ownership</h3>

<p>Businesses should understand what happens when an employee forgets their master password, loses access to a device or leaves the organization unexpectedly.</p>

<p>Review the provider's account recovery process, emergency access options and administrative recovery capabilities. Do not assume that an administrator can automatically decrypt every employee's private vault.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">9. Integrations and scalability</h3>

<p>As a business grows, it may want its password manager to work with identity providers, directory services, single sign-on systems and security monitoring tools.</p>

<p>Even if your business does not need these integrations today, consider whether the platform can support them later without requiring a disruptive migration.</p>

<h2 style="border-bottom: 2px solid rgb(228, 216, 245); color: #45247a; padding-bottom: 9px;">How to Choose the Right Password Manager for Your Small Business</h2>

<p>The best option depends on your team's size, technical resources and security requirements.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">1. Start with your team size</h3>

<p>Count the employees and contractors who need access to business credentials. Check whether a provider has a minimum number of seats or a maximum user limit on its entry-level plan.</p>

<p>A flat-rate package for 10 users may be economical for a 10-person team but less attractive for a business with only three employees.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">2. Identify the accounts that need protection</h3>

<p>Make a list of the business accounts employees use. These might include email, accounting, banking, customer relationship management, cloud storage, social media and website administration.</p>

<p>Determine which accounts require individual access and which credentials must be shared among authorized employees.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">3. Decide how much administrative control you need</h3>

<p>A business with a handful of employees may only need shared vaults and basic user management.</p>

<p>A larger organization may require directory synchronization, role-based access, SSO, automated provisioning and security reporting.</p>

<p>Do not pay for advanced enterprise features simply because they sound impressive. Choose controls that solve actual business requirements.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">4. Review security architecture and recovery</h3>

<p>Read the provider's security documentation. Understand its encryption model, authentication options, account recovery process and incident-response practices.</p>

<p>Also consider what happens if an administrator loses access or an employee leaves with access to shared credentials.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">5. Test the user experience</h3>

<p>Take advantage of free trials where available. Test browser extensions, mobile applications, password sharing, autofill and administrative workflows.</p>

<p>A tool that employees find difficult to use may lead them back to insecure password-sharing habits.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">6. Calculate the cost as your team grows</h3>

<p>Compare what the subscription would cost for your current team and a larger team. Include the cost of the plan that contains the features you actually need.</p>

<p>For example, a low-priced plan may be sufficient for a small team today, but the business may eventually need a more expensive tier for SSO or automated user provisioning.</p>

<h2 style="border-bottom: 2px solid rgb(228, 216, 245); color: #45247a; padding-bottom: 9px;">Password Manager vs. Browser Password Storage: Which Is Better for a Business?</h2>

<p>Modern browsers can save and autofill passwords, which is convenient for individuals. However, business credential management often requires more than storing passwords on a single employee's browser profile.</p>

<p>A dedicated business password manager typically offers additional capabilities such as:</p>

<ul>
<li>Centralized administration of employee accounts.</li>
<li>Secure sharing of credentials between authorized team members.</li>
<li>Organizational vaults and shared folders.</li>
<li>Access policies and employee offboarding controls.</li>
<li>Security reporting and activity visibility.</li>
<li>Integration with business identity and directory systems.</li>
</ul>

<p>Browser-based password storage may be sufficient for some individual users, but businesses with shared accounts and multiple employees generally benefit from a dedicated management system.</p>

<h2 style="border-bottom: 2px solid rgb(228, 216, 245); color: #45247a; padding-bottom: 9px;">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">What is the best password manager for a small business?</h3>
<p>Bitwarden is a strong value option for small businesses that need centralized credential management and secure sharing. 1Password is a compelling alternative for teams that prioritize usability, while Keeper offers a low-cost entry plan for very small teams. The right choice depends on your administrative and security requirements.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">Are business password managers worth the cost?</h3>
<p>They can be. A business password manager helps employees use unique passwords, reduces unsafe credential-sharing practices and gives administrators better control over access. Its value depends on how the business implements it and whether employees consistently use it.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">Can a password manager be used by multiple employees?</h3>
<p>Yes. Business password managers provide organizational features that allow authorized employees to share credentials through vaults, collections or folders. Administrators can usually manage who has access to those shared items.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">Are password managers safe for businesses?</h3>
<p>Reputable password managers use encryption and other security controls to protect stored credentials. However, no software eliminates every risk. Businesses should review the provider's security documentation, enable MFA, protect administrator accounts and establish appropriate access policies.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">Does a password manager replace multifactor authentication?</h3>
<p>No. A password manager helps create and store passwords, while MFA adds another verification factor during authentication. They serve different purposes and should be used together where supported.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">Can a password manager protect a business from phishing?</h3>
<p>Some password managers include features that can help identify suspicious websites or credential risks. However, a password manager does not eliminate phishing. Businesses should also use MFA, employee training, secure email practices and other appropriate security controls.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">What happens when an employee leaves the company?</h3>
<p>Administrators should remove the employee's access to the password manager and any shared vaults. The business should also review accounts the employee could access and rotate shared passwords when necessary.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">Can I use a free password manager for my business?</h3>
<p>Some providers offer free personal plans, but these may lack the centralized administration, sharing controls and employee management features needed by an organization. A dedicated business plan is generally more appropriate when multiple employees need access to company credentials.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">What is the difference between a personal and business password manager?</h3>
<p>A personal password manager focuses on an individual's credentials. A business password manager adds organizational features such as shared vaults, employee accounts, administrative policies, reporting and access management.</p>

<h3 style="color: #65449a;">How much should a small business spend on password management?</h3>
<p>Business plans in this comparison range from approximately $2 to $8.99 per user per month on annual billing, depending on the provider and package. The business should select a plan based on its required features rather than choosing the cheapest subscription automatically.</p>

<li><b>Choose 1Password</b> if you want an approachable user experience, shared vaults and useful business administration.</li>
