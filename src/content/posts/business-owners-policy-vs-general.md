  <h1 style="color: #172554; font-size: 34px; line-height: 1.25; margin: 0px 0px 20px;">Business Owners Policy vs General Liability Insurance: What's the Difference?</h1>

  <p>Choosing business insurance can feel confusing, especially when two policies appear to offer similar protection. General liability insurance and a Business Owners Policy (BOP) are closely related, but they are not interchangeable.</p><p><br /></p><p><img alt="Insurance brokers Images - Free Download on Magnific (formerly Freepik)" class="sFlh5c FyHeAf uT7N3d" height="445" src="https://img.magnific.com/free-photo/happy-man-talking-his-wife-while-signing-agreement-with-insurance-agent-office_637285-6900.jpg?semt=ais_hybrid&amp;w=740&amp;q=80" style="--xgflle: 1.5010141987829615; max-width: 740px; text-align: center;" width="668" /></p><p style="text-align: center;">&nbsp;</p>

  <p>Both can protect your business against certain third-party claims. However, a BOP usually goes further by combining liability protection with commercial property insurance and, in many cases, business interruption coverage.</p>

  <p>The right choice depends on what your business owns, where it operates, the risks it faces and the insurance requirements attached to leases or contracts.</p>

  <div style="background: repeat rgb(239, 246, 255); border-left-color: rgb(37, 99, 235); border-left-style: solid; border-left-width: 5px; border-left: 5px solid rgb(37, 99, 235); border-radius: 5px; margin: 25px 0px; padding: 20px 22px;">
    <h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 20px; margin: 0px 0px 10px;">The short answer</h2>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">Choose <b>general liability insurance</b> if your main concern is protection against third-party claims, such as customer injuries or accidental damage to someone else's property. Consider a <b>Business Owners Policy</b> if you also need to protect business equipment, inventory, furniture, premises or income following a covered interruption.</p>
  </div>

  <h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 27px; margin-top: 38px;">1. What Is General Liability Insurance?</h2>

  <p>General liability insurance, sometimes called commercial general liability (CGL), helps protect a business when it is held legally responsible for certain injuries, property damage or personal and advertising injury involving third parties.</p><p style="text-align: center;"><br /></p><p style="text-align: center;">&nbsp;<img alt="Page 38 | Insurance office Images - Free Download on Magnific (formerly  Freepik)" class="sFlh5c FyHeAf uT7N3d" height="426" src="https://img.magnific.com/free-photo/colleagues-reading-documents-office_482257-115037.jpg?semt=ais_hybrid&amp;w=740&amp;q=80" style="--xgflle: 1.5010141987829615; max-width: 740px;" width="640" /></p>

  <p>For example, imagine a customer visits your shop, slips on a wet floor and suffers an injury. The customer may make a claim against your business. If the incident is covered by your policy, general liability insurance may help pay eligible medical expenses, legal defence costs and settlements or judgments, subject to the policy's terms and limits.</p>

  <p>Other situations that may fall within general liability coverage include:</p>

  <ul style="padding-left: 24px;">
    <li>A contractor accidentally damages a client's property while carrying out a job.</li>
    <li>A customer is injured after using a product sold by your business.</li>
    <li>Your business is accused of causing certain personal or advertising injuries, such as libel or slander.</li>
    <li>A visitor is injured at your business premises and alleges that your business was responsible.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>Coverage depends on the facts of the incident, policy wording, exclusions and applicable limits. Not every accident or lawsuit will be covered.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px; margin-top: 25px;">What general liability insurance does not usually cover</h3>

  <p>A standalone general liability policy is mainly designed for third-party liability risks. It does not normally insure your own business equipment, stock, furniture or building against property damage.</p>

  <p>It also does not automatically cover every type of business-related claim. For example, professional mistakes, employee injuries, business vehicle accidents and cyber incidents generally require separate policies or specific endorsements.</p>

  <p>This distinction matters. A business may have general liability insurance and still face a substantial financial loss if a fire destroys its equipment or a covered event forces it to suspend operations.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 27px; margin-top: 38px;">2. What Is a Business Owners Policy (BOP)?</h2>

  <p>A Business Owners Policy, commonly shortened to BOP, is a package insurance policy designed for eligible small and often lower-risk businesses. It typically combines general liability insurance with commercial property coverage. It may also include business income or business interruption protection.</p>

  <p>Instead of purchasing separate liability and property policies, a qualifying business can obtain these core protections through one package. Bundling may be more cost-effective than buying the coverages separately, although the actual price and savings depend on the insurer and the business's circumstances.</p>

  <p>For example, consider a small retail store. A BOP may help protect the owner if a customer suffers a covered injury in the shop. It may also cover eligible damage to insured stock, equipment or other business property following a covered event. If that event temporarily prevents the business from operating, business income coverage may help replace certain lost income.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px; margin-top: 25px;">The three common parts of a BOP</h3>

  <h4 style="color: #172554; font-size: 18px; margin-bottom: 6px;">General liability coverage</h4>
  <p>This is the liability component of the package. It can help with covered third-party bodily injury, property damage and personal or advertising injury claims.</p>

  <h4 style="color: #172554; font-size: 18px; margin-bottom: 6px;">Commercial property coverage</h4>
  <p>This part can protect insured business property, such as equipment, furniture, inventory and, where included, the building. The policy specifies which property is insured, covered causes of loss, limits, deductibles and exclusions.</p>

  <h4 style="color: #172554; font-size: 18px; margin-bottom: 6px;">Business income or interruption coverage</h4>
  <p>This coverage may help replace lost business income and pay certain continuing operating expenses when a covered property loss forces the business to suspend or reduce operations. It is subject to the policy's conditions, waiting periods, limits and period of restoration.</p>

  <div style="background: repeat rgb(248, 250, 252); border-radius: 6px; border: 1px solid rgb(226, 232, 240); margin: 24px 0px; padding: 18px 20px;">
    <p style="margin: 0px;"><b>Important:</b> A BOP is not a promise to cover every loss. For example, standard commercial property coverage commonly excludes flood damage, and other exclusions may apply. You may need separate flood insurance or additional endorsements depending on your location and exposure.</p>
  </div>

  <h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 27px; margin-top: 38px;">6. Which Businesses Should Consider a BOP?</h2>

  <p>A BOP can be useful for a small business that needs both liability protection and cover for physical business assets. However, not every business qualifies, and not every business needs the same package.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px; margin-top: 25px;">Retail stores and small shops</h3>
  <p>Retail businesses may have customers visiting their premises, inventory to protect and equipment used for daily operations. A BOP can combine liability and property protection in one package.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px; margin-top: 25px;">Small restaurants and cafés</h3>
  <p>Restaurants and cafés may need protection for customer injury claims as well as insured equipment, furniture and stock. Their actual coverage needs can be more specialised, so owners should confirm that the insurer will cover their particular operations.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px; margin-top: 25px;">Small offices and consulting businesses</h3>
  <p>An office-based business may have computers, furniture and other equipment to insure. However, consultants and professional service providers should also consider professional liability insurance because a BOP does not normally replace it.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px; margin-top: 25px;">Home-based businesses</h3>
  <p>A home-based business may qualify for a BOP, depending on its operations and the insurer's eligibility rules. Standard homeowners insurance may not adequately cover business property or business-related liability, so owners should not assume their household policy provides sufficient protection.</p>

  <div style="background: repeat rgb(239, 246, 255); border-left-color: rgb(37, 99, 235); border-left-style: solid; border-left-width: 5px; border-left: 5px solid rgb(37, 99, 235); border-radius: 5px; margin: 25px 0px; padding: 18px 20px;">
    <p style="margin: 0px;"><b>Eligibility warning:</b> BOPs are generally designed for eligible small and lower-risk businesses. Larger operations, manufacturers and businesses with unusual or specialised risks may need a customised commercial insurance package instead.</p>
  </div>

  <h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 27px; margin-top: 38px;">7. When Is General Liability Insurance Enough?</h2>

  <p>There are situations where a standalone general liability policy may be a reasonable starting point.</p>

  <p>For example, a self-employed contractor or service provider may have relatively few physical business assets to insure but still interact with clients, visit customer premises or sign contracts requiring liability coverage.</p>

  <p>In such cases, general liability may address an important part of the business's exposure without requiring a property package that the owner does not currently need.</p>

  <p>However, a business should not choose general liability simply because it is cheaper. Consider whether you could afford to replace your equipment, inventory or other assets after a major covered loss. Also check whether your lease, clients or contracts require particular coverage.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 27px; margin-top: 38px;">8. What a BOP Does Not Automatically Cover</h2>

  <p>One of the most common mistakes business owners make is assuming that a BOP is an all-in-one insurance solution. It is not.</p>

  <p>Depending on the insurer and policy, you may need separate insurance or additional endorsements for:</p>

  <ul style="padding-left: 24px;">
    <li><b>Workers' compensation:</b> Covers eligible work-related employee injuries and illnesses under applicable state laws. Requirements differ by state and business circumstances.</li>
    <li><b>Commercial auto:</b> Covers specified vehicles and business driving exposures that are not ordinarily insured by a BOP.</li>
    <li><b>Professional liability:</b> Addresses certain claims involving professional services, mistakes or negligence.</li>
    <li><b>Cyber liability:</b> Helps address covered expenses and claims arising from cyber incidents and data breaches.</li>
    <li><b>Flood insurance:</b> Standard commercial property coverage commonly excludes flood damage, so separate protection may be necessary.</li>
    <li><b>Commercial umbrella insurance:</b> Can provide additional liability limits above certain underlying policies, subject to its terms.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 27px; margin-top: 38px;">9. How to Choose Between a BOP and General Liability Insurance</h2>

  <p>Before requesting quotes, work through these questions:</p>

  <h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 20px;">1. Do you own business property that needs protection?</h3>
  <p>List your equipment, stock, furniture, tools and other physical assets. If replacing them after a covered loss would seriously affect your finances, property coverage may be important.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 20px;">2. Could a covered property loss interrupt your operations?</h3>
  <p>Consider whether your business could continue operating if your premises or essential equipment became unusable. Business income coverage may be valuable if a covered interruption would leave you unable to generate revenue while expenses continue.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 20px;">3. Do clients, landlords or contracts require liability insurance?</h3>
  <p>Review your lease agreements, service contracts and customer requirements. Check the required limits and whether additional insured status or other policy provisions are required.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 20px;">4. Does your business have risks that require separate policies?</h3>
  <p>A BOP may not address professional services, employee injuries, business vehicles or cyber incidents. Identify these exposures before assuming a package policy is sufficient.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 20px;">5. Compare quotes with equivalent coverage</h3>
  <p>Request quotes for a standalone general liability policy and a BOP where both are available. Compare liability limits, property limits, deductibles, covered causes of loss, business income provisions and exclusions. A lower premium may simply reflect less protection.</p>

  <div style="border-radius: 6px; border: 1px solid rgb(226, 232, 240); margin: 15px 0px; padding: 18px;">
    <h3 style="color: #172554; font-size: 18px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">Does a BOP cover flood damage?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">Standard commercial property coverage commonly excludes flood damage. Businesses with flood exposure should ask an insurance professional whether separate flood coverage is available and appropriate.</p>
  </div>

  <div style="border-radius: 6px; border: 1px solid rgb(226, 232, 240); margin: 15px 0px; padding: 18px;">
    <h3 style="color: #172554; font-size: 18px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">Can I have general liability insurance and a BOP at the same time?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">A BOP already typically includes general liability coverage. Buying another standalone general liability policy may create overlapping coverage rather than useful additional protection. Discuss the proposed policies with a licensed insurance professional before purchasing both.</p>
  </div>

  <div style="background: repeat rgb(23, 37, 84); border-radius: 7px; color: white; margin: 35px 0px; padding: 25px 26px;">
    <h2 style="color: white; font-size: 24px; margin: 0px 0px 12px;">Final Verdict: Which Policy Should Your Business Choose?</h2>
    <p style="color: #e2e8f0; margin: 0px 0px 13px;">General liability insurance and a Business Owners Policy serve related but different purposes. General liability focuses on certain third-party claims. A BOP typically combines that protection with insurance for eligible business property and business income losses following covered interruptions.</p>
    <p style="color: #e2e8f0; margin: 0px 0px 13px;">If your business has valuable equipment, inventory or premises to protect, a BOP may offer a more suitable starting point. If you mainly need third-party liability protection and have limited business property exposure, standalone general liability may be worth considering.</p>
    <p style="color: white; font-weight: bold; margin: 0px;">Do not choose based on price alone. Compare the risks you need to insure, the policy limits, exclusions and deductibles, and confirm that the coverage meets your contractual and legal requirements.</p>
  </div>
