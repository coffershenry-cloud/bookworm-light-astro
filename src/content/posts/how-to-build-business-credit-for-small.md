  <h2 id="business-credit" style="border-bottom: 2px solid rgb(238, 232, 250); color: #1d2c43; font-size: 26px; line-height: 1.35; margin: 36px 0px 17px; padding-bottom: 10px;">What Is Business Credit?</h2>

  <p>
    Business credit is a record of how a company manages financial obligations, including payments to suppliers, lenders and other creditors. Commercial credit reporting agencies collect information about businesses and use it to produce credit reports, scores and risk assessments.
  </p>

  <p>
    A business credit report may contain information such as:
  </p>

  <ul style="padding-left: 23px;">
    <li>Business name, address and identifying details.</li>
    <li>Company registration and public record information.</li>
    <li>Payment history reported by suppliers and lenders.</li>
    <li>Outstanding balances and credit obligations.</li>
    <li>Collections, liens, judgments or other relevant public records.</li>
    <li>Business credit scores and risk indicators.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    Lenders, suppliers, landlords, insurers and potential business partners may use commercial credit information when assessing whether to extend credit, offer payment terms or enter into a business relationship.
  </p>

  <p>
    For example, a small retail company that regularly purchases inventory from suppliers may eventually qualify for trade credit. Instead of paying for every order immediately, the supplier may allow the business to pay an invoice within an agreed period. If the supplier reports that payment activity to a commercial credit bureau, the account may contribute to the company's business credit history.
  </p>

  <div style="background: rgb(245, 247, 251); border-radius: 9px; margin: 22px 0px; padding: 18px 20px;">
    <h3 style="color: #263b59; font-size: 18px; margin: 0px 0px 9px;">Important distinction</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">
      Having a registered business, an EIN or a business bank account does not automatically mean that your company has a strong business credit score. These steps help establish a business identity. Credit history generally develops when creditors and suppliers report relevant financial activity to commercial credit reporting agencies.
    </p>
  </div>

  <p>
    Personal credit describes an individual's borrowing and repayment history. Business credit relates to the financial activity and credit profile of a company.
  </p>

  <p>
    Although they are different, they are not always completely independent. When a small business is new or has limited financial history, lenders may consider the owner's personal credit when deciding whether to approve financing.
  </p>

    A small business may operate successfully for years using its revenue and personal funding. However, as the company grows, it may need access to supplier credit, equipment financing, working capital or other commercial financial products.
  </p>

  <p>
    Establishing business credit before you urgently need financing can help create a record that potential creditors and suppliers can evaluate.

  <h3 style="color: #354a68; font-size: 21px; margin: 27px 0px 10px;">1. Establish a Consistent Business Identity</h3>

  <p>
    Before applying for business credit, make sure your company has a consistent identity that creditors, suppliers and reporting agencies can recognize.
  </p>

  <p>
    If you operate an LLC or corporation, use the legal business name shown on your formation documents. Keep your registered address, business phone number and other identifying information consistent across your bank account, tax records, licenses, supplier applications and credit applications.
  </p>

  <p>
    If you operate as a sole proprietor, you may still be able to establish a business credit profile. However, your business is not legally separate from you in the same way as an LLC or corporation, and lenders may rely more heavily on your personal credit.
  </p>

  <p>
    Consider the following:
  </p>

  <ul style="padding-left: 23px;">
    <li>Choose and consistently use your legal business name.</li>
    <li>Register your business with the appropriate state or local authority when required.</li>
    <li>Use a consistent business address and telephone number.</li>
    <li>Keep business licenses and registration records up to date.</li>
    <li>Use the same business information when opening accounts and applying for credit.</li>
  </ul>

  <div style="background: rgb(245, 247, 251); border-radius: 9px; margin: 19px 0px; padding: 17px 19px;">
    <h3 style="color: #263b59; font-size: 17px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">Why this matters</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">
      Inconsistent business names, addresses or other identifying details can make it harder for reporting agencies to match financial information to the correct company. Check your business information before opening new accounts.
    </p>
  </div>

  <h3 style="color: #354a68; font-size: 21px; margin: 27px 0px 10px;">2. Get an Employer Identification Number (EIN)</h3>

  <p>
    An Employer Identification Number is a federal tax identification number assigned by the Internal Revenue Service. It identifies a business entity for tax and other business purposes.
  </p>

  <p>
    An EIN is required for many businesses, including corporations and partnerships, and businesses generally need one when hiring employees or meeting certain federal tax obligations. Some sole proprietors may not be required to obtain one for federal tax purposes, but an EIN can still be useful for business banking and other applications.
  </p>

  <p>
    For eligible applicants with a principal place of business in the United States or its territories, the IRS provides a free online EIN application. If approved, the number is issued immediately online.
  </p>

  <p>
    <a href="https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/get-an-employer-identification-number" rel="nofollow noopener" style="color: #6741b8;" target="_blank">Apply for an EIN directly through the IRS</a>.
  </p>

  <div style="background: rgb(255, 248, 235); border-color: rgb(241, 223, 185) rgb(241, 223, 185) rgb(241, 223, 185) rgb(216, 155, 54); border-image: none; border-left: 5px solid #d89b36; border-radius: 9px; border-style: solid; border-width: 1px 1px 1px 5px; border: 1px solid #f1dfb9; margin: 22px 0px; padding: 18px 20px;">
    <h3 style="color: #765019; font-size: 18px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">Avoid paying unnecessary EIN fees</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">
      The IRS does not charge a fee to issue an EIN. Be careful with websites that charge simply to submit an EIN application on your behalf. Use the official IRS website instead.
    </p>
  </div>

  <h3 style="color: #354a68; font-size: 21px; margin: 27px 0px 10px;">3. Request a D-U-N-S Number</h3>

  <p>
    A D-U-N-S Number is a unique nine-digit business identifier issued by Dun &amp; Bradstreet. It helps identify a company within Dun &amp; Bradstreet's business information system.
  </p>

  <p>
    Some lenders, suppliers and business partners may use a D-U-N-S Number when searching for a company's business credit information. It can therefore be a useful part of establishing your business identity.
  </p>

  <p>
    Dun &amp; Bradstreet provides a free D-U-N-S Number request option. The company also offers paid products and services, but you do not need to purchase an optional monitoring or credit-building subscription simply to request the free identifier.
  </p>

  <p>
    <a href="https://www.dnb.com/duns-number/get-a-duns.html" rel="nofollow noopener" style="color: #6741b8;" target="_blank">Request a D-U-N-S Number from Dun &amp; Bradstreet</a>.
  </p>

  <p>
    After receiving the number, check that the business details associated with the profile are accurate. A D-U-N-S Number identifies the business, but it does not by itself create a positive payment history or guarantee a PAYDEX score.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #354a68; font-size: 21px; margin: 27px 0px 10px;">4. Open a Dedicated Business Bank Account</h3>

  <p>
    A dedicated business bank account helps separate company income and expenses from your personal transactions. It also creates a clearer financial record that can be useful when applying for commercial credit.
  </p>

  <p>
    Many banks request business registration documents, an EIN or other identifying information when opening an account. Requirements differ depending on the bank and business structure.
  </p>

  <p>
    Use the account for normal business activity, such as receiving customer payments, paying suppliers and managing operating expenses. Avoid mixing personal and business spending whenever possible.
  </p>

  <p>
    Remember that opening a business checking account does not automatically create a business credit score. Its value is that it helps establish financial separation and gives lenders a clearer view of your company's banking activity when they request it.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #354a68; font-size: 21px; margin: 27px 0px 10px;">5. Open Business Accounts That Report Payment History</h3>

  <p>
    This is one of the most important steps in building business credit. Your company needs financial accounts whose payment activity is reported to one or more commercial credit reporting agencies.
  </p>

  <p>
    One option is a supplier account that allows your business to purchase goods or services and pay later under agreed terms. This arrangement is often called trade credit.
  </p>

  <p>
    For example, a small cleaning company may regularly purchase supplies from a distributor. If the supplier offers invoice terms and reports payment experiences to a commercial credit bureau, the account may help establish a record of the company's payment behaviour.
  </p>

  <p>
    Other possible credit products include:
  </p>

  <ul style="padding-left: 23px;">
    <li><b>Vendor trade accounts:</b> Credit arrangements with suppliers that allow the business to pay invoices later.</li>
    <li><b>Business credit cards:</b> Cards used for business purchases, provided the issuer reports the relevant activity to commercial credit bureaus.</li>
    <li><b>Business lines of credit:</b> Revolving credit facilities that may report account activity, depending on the lender.</li>
    <li><b>Equipment financing:</b> Financing used to purchase eligible business equipment, where the lender reports payment information.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    Not every supplier, lender or card issuer reports to every business credit bureau. Some may report only negative information, while others may report to one bureau but not the others.
  </p>

  <p>
    Before opening an account, ask the provider:
  </p>

  <ul style="padding-left: 23px;">
    <li>Do you report payment activity to business credit bureaus?</li>
    <li>Which bureaus receive the information?</li>
    <li>Do you report positive payments, late payments or both?</li>
    <li>How frequently is payment information submitted?</li>
    <li>Are there fees, minimum purchases or other account requirements?</li>
  </ul>

  <div style="background: rgb(245, 247, 251); border-radius: 9px; margin: 22px 0px; padding: 18px 20px;">
    <h3 style="color: #263b59; font-size: 18px; margin: 0px 0px 9px;">Do not open accounts just to chase a score</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">
      Only use credit for purchases your business genuinely needs and can afford to repay. Opening unnecessary accounts, paying avoidable fees or buying products you do not need can create costs without providing meaningful business value.
    </p>
  </div>

  <h3 style="color: #354a68; font-size: 21px; margin: 27px 0px 10px;">6. Pay Business Bills on Time or Early</h3>

  <p>
    Once your business has accounts that report payment activity, manage them responsibly. Payment history is an important part of many commercial credit assessments.
  </p>

  <p>
    Pay supplier invoices, business credit card bills and other obligations by their due dates. If your cash flow allows it, paying early may be beneficial under certain commercial scoring models.
  </p>

  <p>
    For example, Dun &amp; Bradstreet's PAYDEX Score uses reported payment experiences to assess how promptly a business pays its suppliers. The score is based on the payment information Dun &amp; Bradstreet receives, not simply on whether the business has an account.
  </p>

  <p>
    You can make payment management easier by:
  </p>

  <ul style="padding-left: 23px;">
    <li>Keeping a calendar of invoice due dates.</li>
    <li>Setting up payment reminders.</li>
    <li>Using automatic payments where appropriate and where sufficient funds are available.</li>
    <li>Reviewing invoices for errors before paying.</li>
    <li>Maintaining enough working capital to meet upcoming obligations.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    A single late payment may not affect every scoring model in the same way, but repeated late payments can damage the impression your business creates with creditors and suppliers.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #354a68; font-size: 21px; margin: 27px 0px 10px;">7. Monitor Your Business Credit Reports and Correct Errors</h3>

  <p>
    Building business credit is an ongoing process. Your company information and reported payment history may change as suppliers submit new information, accounts are opened or closed and public records are updated.
  </p>

  <p>
    Check your business credit information periodically to make sure:
  </p>

  <ul style="padding-left: 23px;">
    <li>Your legal business name and address are correct.</li>
    <li>Your business is not being confused with another company.</li>
    <li>Accounts you recognize are listed accurately.</li>
    <li>Payment information reflects what actually happened.</li>
    <li>Incorrect balances, duplicate accounts or outdated information are addressed.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    If you find an error, follow the reporting agency's process for requesting an investigation or correction. Keep supporting documents such as paid invoices, account statements and correspondence with suppliers.
  </p>

    The three names small-business owners are most likely to encounter are Dun &amp; Bradstreet, Experian Business and Equifax Business.

  <h3 style="color: #354a68; font-size: 20px; margin: 24px 0px 9px;">Dun &amp; Bradstreet</h3>

  <p>
    Dun &amp; Bradstreet maintains business information and commercial credit profiles. One of its best-known scoring products is the PAYDEX Score, which evaluates reported payment experiences with suppliers and creditors.
  </p>

  <p>
    The PAYDEX Score generally uses a scale from 1 to 100. A score of 80 is commonly associated with paying according to agreed terms, while higher scores can indicate that a business pays earlier than the agreed terms. The score is based on reported payment data, so it is important to confirm that suppliers actually submit information to Dun &amp; Bradstreet.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #354a68; font-size: 20px; margin: 24px 0px 9px;">Experian Business</h3>

    <p style="margin: 0px;">
      Each bureau may receive different payment information and use its own scoring model. Your business may therefore have a profile or score with one agency but limited information with another. Do not assume that one score represents your company's complete commercial credit position.

  <h2 id="tradelines" style="border-bottom: 2px solid rgb(238, 232, 250); color: #1d2c43; font-size: 26px; line-height: 1.35; margin: 40px 0px 17px; padding-bottom: 10px;">What Are Business Tradelines?</h2>

  <p>
    A business tradeline is an account or credit relationship that may appear on a company's commercial credit report. Examples include supplier credit accounts, business credit cards, loans and other eligible credit arrangements.
  </p>

  <p>
    A tradeline can contain information such as the creditor's name, account details, credit limit, balance and payment history, depending on what the creditor reports and what the bureau includes in its report.
  </p>

  <p>
    For a small business, trade credit can be a practical way to establish payment history because it may arise from purchases the company already needs to make.
  </p>

  <p>
    However, the word "tradeline" does not automatically mean an account will build credit. The provider must report relevant information to the bureau, and the bureau must be able to match the account to the correct business.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #354a68; font-size: 20px; margin: 25px 0px 9px;">Should a Small Business Open Net-30 Accounts?</h3>

  <p>
    A net-30 account is a supplier arrangement that generally requires an invoice to be paid within 30 days. Some suppliers offer these terms to business customers, subject to their own approval requirements.
  </p>

  <p>
    A net-30 account may be useful when:
  </p>

  <ul style="padding-left: 23px;">
    <li>Your business regularly buys products or supplies from the vendor.</li>
    <li>The supplier offers reasonable prices and clear payment terms.</li>
    <li>The account reports eligible payment information to a commercial bureau.</li>
    <li>Your business can comfortably pay the invoice by the due date.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    Do not open several net-30 accounts simply because a website claims they can create business credit quickly. Some vendors do not report positive payments, and some credit-building offers may involve fees or purchasing requirements that are not worthwhile for your business.
  </p>

  <h2 id="timeline" style="border-bottom: 2px solid rgb(238, 232, 250); color: #1d2c43; font-size: 26px; line-height: 1.35; margin: 40px 0px 17px; padding-bottom: 10px;">How Long Does It Take to Build Business Credit?</h2>

  <p>
    There is no fixed timeline for building business credit. The time required depends on when your business begins using accounts that report, how frequently creditors submit payment information and how much history the reporting agency needs to assess your company.
  </p>

  <p>
    Some businesses may begin to establish a credit file after receiving their first reported account information. Developing a more useful payment history, however, takes longer because reporting agencies and lenders need enough information to assess the business.
  </p>

  <p>
    A newly established company should not expect a strong credit profile immediately after receiving an EIN or D-U-N-S Number. These identifiers help establish the business's identity, but they are not substitutes for reported payment history.
  </p>

  <p>
    A practical approach is to focus on the steps you can control:
  </p>

  <ul style="padding-left: 23px;">
    <li>Make sure your business information is accurate.</li>
    <li>Open suitable accounts that report payment activity.</li>
    <li>Pay invoices according to their terms.</li>
    <li>Check that reported information appears on the correct business profile.</li>
    <li>Continue monitoring and maintaining the accounts over time.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    Be cautious of companies promising instant business credit, guaranteed high scores or large loans without meaningful underwriting. Legitimate credit histories depend on actual financial activity and reporting.
  </p>

  <h2 id="mistakes" style="border-bottom: 2px solid rgb(238, 232, 250); color: #1d2c43; font-size: 26px; line-height: 1.35; margin: 40px 0px 17px; padding-bottom: 10px;">Business Credit Mistakes Small Businesses Should Avoid</h2>

  <p><img alt="Stressed business man Images - Free Download on Magnific (formerly Freepik)" class="sFlh5c Jm0Uqe FyHeAf uT7N3d" height="426" src="https://img.magnific.com/free-photo/upset-young-businessman-sitting-workplace-office-background_176420-5291.jpg?semt=ais_hybrid&amp;w=740&amp;q=80" style="--xgflle: 1.5010141987829615; max-width: 740px;" width="640" />&nbsp;</p><p>Establishing business credit is not just about opening accounts. The way you manage those accounts and the accuracy of your business information matter as well.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p>

  <h3 style="color: #354a68; font-size: 19px; margin: 23px 0px 8px;">1. Assuming an EIN Automatically Creates Business Credit</h3>
  <p>
    An EIN identifies a business for federal tax purposes. It does not automatically establish a payment history or guarantee a business credit score.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #354a68; font-size: 19px; margin: 23px 0px 8px;">2. Applying for Accounts That Do Not Report</h3>
  <p>
    A supplier may offer credit without reporting positive payment activity to commercial credit bureaus. Ask about its reporting practices before relying on the account as a credit-building tool.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #354a68; font-size: 19px; margin: 23px 0px 8px;">3. Missing Payment Due Dates</h3>
  <p>
    Late payments can damage your relationship with suppliers and may negatively affect reported payment history. Use reminders and cash-flow planning to avoid missed invoices.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #354a68; font-size: 19px; margin: 23px 0px 8px;">4. Mixing Personal and Business Finances</h3>
  <p>
    Using personal accounts for business purchases can make bookkeeping and financial assessments more complicated. Keep company transactions organized and use dedicated business accounts where appropriate.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #354a68; font-size: 19px; margin: 23px 0px 8px;">5. Taking on Debt You Do Not Need</h3>
  <p>
    Borrowing money solely to create credit history can expose your business to interest charges, fees and repayment pressure. Only use credit when the purchase supports a genuine business need and the repayment is affordable.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #354a68; font-size: 19px; margin: 23px 0px 8px;">6. Ignoring Personal Credit</h3>
  <p>
    Many small-business lenders still consider an owner's personal credit, especially when the company has limited financial history. Continue managing personal obligations responsibly while building the company's commercial profile.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #354a68; font-size: 19px; margin: 23px 0px 8px;">7. Paying for Unnecessary Credit-Building Promises</h3>
  <p>
    Be careful with services that promise guaranteed business credit scores, instant approvals or special tradelines for a fee. Check what the service actually provides, whether it reports genuine payment activity and whether the cost is justified.
  </p>

  <h2 id="faqs" style="border-bottom: 2px solid rgb(238, 232, 250); color: #1d2c43; font-size: 26px; line-height: 1.35; margin: 40px 0px 17px; padding-bottom: 10px;">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

  <div style="border-bottom: 1px solid rgb(231, 233, 239); padding: 17px 0px;">
    <h3 style="color: #263b59; font-size: 18px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">1. How do I build business credit for a small business?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">Establish a consistent business identity, obtain an EIN when appropriate, request a D-U-N-S Number, open a business bank account and use suitable credit accounts that report payment activity to commercial credit bureaus. Pay obligations on time and monitor your business credit reports for errors.</p>
  </div>

  <div style="border-bottom: 1px solid rgb(231, 233, 239); padding: 17px 0px;">
    <h3 style="color: #263b59; font-size: 18px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">2. Can I build business credit without an LLC?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">Yes. A sole proprietor may be able to establish a business credit profile using consistent business information and accounts that report to commercial bureaus. However, a sole proprietorship is not legally separate from its owner in the same way as an LLC or corporation, and lenders may rely more heavily on personal credit.</p>
  </div>

  <div style="border-bottom: 1px solid rgb(231, 233, 239); padding: 17px 0px;">
    <h3 style="color: #263b59; font-size: 18px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">3. Is a D-U-N-S Number the same as an EIN?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">No. An EIN is a federal tax identification number issued by the IRS. A D-U-N-S Number is a business identifier issued by Dun &amp; Bradstreet. They serve different purposes and are not interchangeable.</p>
  </div>

  <div style="border-bottom: 1px solid rgb(231, 233, 239); padding: 17px 0px;">
    <h3 style="color: #263b59; font-size: 18px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">4. Does getting an EIN automatically build business credit?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">No. An EIN identifies a business for tax purposes, but it does not automatically create a positive commercial credit history. Your company needs relevant financial activity reported to business credit reporting agencies.</p>
  </div>

  <div style="border-bottom: 1px solid rgb(231, 233, 239); padding: 17px 0px;">
    <h3 style="color: #263b59; font-size: 18px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">5. How long does it take to establish business credit?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">There is no guaranteed timeline. A business may begin developing a file after its first reported account activity, but building a useful payment history requires time and consistent reporting. The timing depends on the creditors, bureaus and amount of information available.</p>
  </div>

  <div style="border-bottom: 1px solid rgb(231, 233, 239); padding: 17px 0px;">
    <h3 style="color: #263b59; font-size: 18px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">6. Can I build business credit with net-30 accounts?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">Potentially. Net-30 supplier accounts may help build business credit if the supplier reports eligible payment information to a commercial credit bureau. Confirm the reporting policy before opening the account.</p>
  </div>

  <div style="border-bottom: 1px solid rgb(231, 233, 239); padding: 17px 0px;">
    <h3 style="color: #263b59; font-size: 18px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">7. Does business credit replace personal credit?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">Not necessarily. Many small-business lenders still review the owner's personal credit or require a personal guarantee, particularly when the business has limited financial history. Building business credit can strengthen your company's profile, but it does not automatically remove personal responsibility for business debt.</p>
  </div>

  <div style="border-bottom: 1px solid rgb(231, 233, 239); padding: 17px 0px;">
    <h3 style="color: #263b59; font-size: 18px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">8. Which business credit bureau is the most important?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">There is no single bureau that is most important for every lender or supplier. Dun &amp; Bradstreet, Experian Business and Equifax Business maintain different commercial credit information and scoring products. The bureau used depends on the organization assessing your business.</p>
  </div>

  <div style="border-bottom: 1px solid rgb(231, 233, 239); padding: 17px 0px;">
    <h3 style="color: #263b59; font-size: 18px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">9. Can a new business get a business credit card?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">Some issuers offer cards to newer businesses, but approval requirements vary. The issuer may consider the owner's personal credit, business revenue, time in business and other financial information. Some cards require a personal guarantee.</p>
  </div>

  <div style="border-bottom: 1px solid rgb(231, 233, 239); padding: 17px 0px;">
    <h3 style="color: #263b59; font-size: 18px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">10. Can I get business credit with bad personal credit?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">It may be possible, but options can be more limited. Some suppliers and credit providers assess business payment history and other company information, while many lenders still review personal credit. Building a separate business profile may help over time, but it is not an immediate workaround for a weak personal credit history.</p>
  </div>

  <div style="border-bottom: 1px solid rgb(231, 233, 239); padding: 17px 0px;">
    <h3 style="color: #263b59; font-size: 18px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">11. Do all vendors report to business credit bureaus?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">No. Some vendors do not report payment information, and others report only to selected agencies or report negative information only. Ask each supplier which bureau receives its payment data and whether positive payment activity is included.</p>
  </div>

  <h2 style="border-bottom: 2px solid rgb(238, 232, 250); color: #1d2c43; font-size: 26px; line-height: 1.35; margin: 40px 0px 17px; padding-bottom: 10px;">Final Verdict: Build Business Credit Before You Need It</h2>

  <p>
    Business credit can become a valuable part of a small company's financial foundation. It gives lenders, suppliers and other commercial partners another way to assess how the business manages its financial obligations.
  </p>

  <p>
    Start by establishing a consistent business identity, obtaining the appropriate identification numbers and separating company finances. Then focus on accounts that genuinely suit your operations and report payment information to commercial credit bureaus.
  </p>

  <p>
    Most importantly, manage those accounts responsibly. Pay invoices on time, avoid unnecessary borrowing, review your business credit reports and correct inaccurate information. Building a useful credit profile takes consistent financial activity, not shortcuts or guaranteed-score promises.
  </p>
