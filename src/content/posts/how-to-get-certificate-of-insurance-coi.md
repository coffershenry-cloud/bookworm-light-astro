  <p>You have secured a new client, won a contract or found the right commercial space for your business. Then the other party asks you to provide a <b>certificate of insurance (COI)</b> before you can proceed.</p>

  <p>If this is your first time dealing with one, you may be wondering where to get it, whether you need to pay for it and how quickly you can receive it.</p>

  <p>The process is usually straightforward. If your business already has an active insurance policy, you can generally request a certificate from your insurance agent, broker or carrier. Depending on the provider, you may be able to download it through an online account or receive it by email.</p>

  <p>However, there is an important detail to understand: a certificate of insurance does not create insurance coverage or change the terms of your policy. If a client requires additional protection, such as additional insured status, that may require a separate policy endorsement.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #123e4e; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 35px;">1. What Is a Certificate of Insurance?</h2><h2 style="color: #123e4e; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 35px;"><img alt="Insurance agency Images - Free Download on Magnific (formerly Freepik)" class="sFlh5c Jm0Uqe FyHeAf uT7N3d" height="426" src="https://img.magnific.com/free-photo/closeup-unrecognizable-couple-signing-contract-with-financial-advisor_637285-11092.jpg?semt=ais_hybrid&amp;w=740&amp;q=80" style="--xgflle: 1.5010141987829615; max-width: 740px;" width="640" />&nbsp;</h2><p>A certificate of insurance is a document that summarizes important information about an insurance policy. It is commonly used to show another party that your business has certain types of insurance in place.</p>

  <p>A COI typically identifies:</p>

  <ul>
    <li>Your business name and contact information.</li>
    <li>The insurance company providing coverage.</li>
    <li>The types of insurance policies you carry.</li>
    <li>Policy numbers and effective dates.</li>
    <li>Coverage limits.</li>
    <li>The certificate holder, if one is specified.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>In the United States, businesses commonly receive a certificate using the <b>ACORD 25</b> form, a standardized certificate of liability insurance used across the insurance industry.</p>

  <p>The document gives a client, landlord, general contractor or other requesting party a convenient summary of the insurance information relevant to its business relationship with you.</p>

  <div style="background: rgb(245, 247, 248); border-radius: 10px; border: 1px solid rgb(224, 231, 233); margin: 22px 0px; padding: 18px;">
    <b style="color: #123e4e;">A simple example</b>
    <p style="margin: 8px 0px 0px;">Imagine a small construction company wins a renovation contract. The property owner wants evidence that the contractor carries general liability insurance before work begins.</p>
    <p style="margin: 8px 0px 0px;">The contractor contacts its insurance agent, requests a COI and provides the property owner's name and address. The agent issues the certificate showing the relevant policy information.</p>
  </div>

  <h2 style="color: #123e4e; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;">2. When Does a Small Business Need a COI?</h2><h2 style="color: #123e4e; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 35px;"><img alt="Insurance agent Images - Free Download on Magnific (formerly Freepik)" class="sFlh5c Jm0Uqe FyHeAf uT7N3d" height="426" src="https://img.magnific.com/free-photo/young-happy-couple-signing-contract-while-being-meeting-with-insurance-agent-office_637285-2828.jpg?semt=ais_hybrid&amp;w=740&amp;q=80" style="--xgflle: 1.5010141987829615; max-width: 740px;" width="640" /></h2><h2 style="color: #123e4e; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;">&nbsp;</h2>

  <p>You may need a certificate whenever another party wants evidence that your business carries insurance before entering into a contract or allowing you to perform work.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">When Signing a Commercial Lease</h3>

  <p>A landlord may require your business to provide proof of general liability insurance before you occupy a commercial property.</p>

  <p>The lease may specify minimum liability limits and require the landlord to be included as an additional insured under your policy. It may also require property insurance for your own equipment, inventory or other business property.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">When Working With a General Contractor</h3>

  <p>General contractors often request COIs from subcontractors before allowing them onto a construction site.</p>

  <p>For example, an electrician, plumber, roofer or HVAC contractor may be asked to provide evidence of general liability insurance and workers' compensation coverage where applicable.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">When Bidding for a Business Contract</h3>

  <p>Some clients want proof of insurance before accepting a proposal or awarding a project.</p>

  <p>This is common when your work involves entering another company's premises, handling its property, providing professional services or interacting with its customers.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">When Providing Services to Another Business</h3>

  <p>A company hiring a consultant, marketing agency, cleaning service, IT provider or other professional may require proof of relevant insurance.</p>

  <p>Depending on the work, the client could request general liability, professional liability (errors and omissions), cyber liability or other coverage.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">When Supplying Products or Services at an Event</h3>

  <p>Event organizers, venues and trade-show operators may ask vendors, caterers, exhibitors and service providers to provide a COI before participating.</p>

  <p>The certificate helps the organizer review the vendor's reported insurance information and determine whether it meets the event's requirements.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #123e4e; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;">3. How to Get a Certificate of Insurance: Step by Step</h2>

  <p>Getting a COI generally involves three main stages: confirming the requirements, checking your policy and requesting the document.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">Step 1: Find Out What the Requesting Party Requires</h3>

  <p>Start by reviewing the contract, lease, vendor agreement or written instructions you received.</p>

  <p>Do not simply ask your insurance agent for a generic certificate without first checking what the other party wants to see.</p>

  <p>Look for requirements involving:</p>

  <ul>
    <li><b>Types of insurance:</b> General liability, commercial auto, workers' compensation, professional liability or other policies.</li>
    <li><b>Coverage limits:</b> Minimum amounts of insurance the client expects you to carry.</li>
    <li><b>Additional insured status:</b> Whether the client or landlord must receive additional insured protection under your policy.</li>
    <li><b>Waiver of subrogation:</b> Whether a policy endorsement waiving certain recovery rights is required.</li>
    <li><b>Policy dates:</b> Whether coverage must remain active throughout a project, lease or contract.</li>
    <li><b>Certificate holder details:</b> The exact legal name and address that must appear on the certificate.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>If the requirements are unclear, ask the requesting party to explain them before contacting your insurer.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">Step 2: Review Your Existing Insurance Policy</h3>

  <p>Once you understand the requirements, compare them with the insurance your business already carries.</p>

  <p>For example, suppose a client requires:</p>

  <ul>
    <li>General liability coverage of at least $1 million per occurrence.</li>
    <li>A $2 million general aggregate limit.</li>
    <li>Additional insured status for the client.</li>
    <li>Coverage throughout the entire project.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>If your policy meets the limits but does not include the required additional insured endorsement, you may need to request a policy change before the certificate can accurately reflect the client's requirements.</p>

  <p>Similarly, if you do not carry a required type of insurance, requesting a COI will not solve the problem. You may need to purchase an additional policy or arrange an appropriate endorsement.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">Step 3: Contact Your Insurance Agent, Broker or Carrier</h3>

  <p>Once you have confirmed that your policy meets the requirements, contact the professional or company that manages your insurance.</p>

  <p>You can usually request a certificate through one of these channels:</p>

  <ul>
    <li><b>Insurance agent:</b> Email or call your agent and request a COI.</li>
    <li><b>Insurance broker:</b> Contact the brokerage that arranged your coverage.</li>
    <li><b>Insurance company:</b> Contact the carrier directly if it handles certificate requests.</li>
    <li><b>Online account:</b> Log in to your insurer's or broker's customer portal if certificate downloads are supported.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>Some providers allow customers to generate certificates online, while others require an agent or service representative to prepare and issue the document.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">Step 4: Provide the Required Information</h3>

  <p>To avoid delays, provide the correct information when making your request.</p>

  <p>In most cases, you should be ready to supply:</p>

  <ul>
    <li>Your business's legal name.</li>
    <li>Your policy number, if available.</li>
    <li>The certificate holder's full legal name.</li>
    <li>The certificate holder's mailing address.</li>
    <li>The contract, project or location description, if required.</li>
    <li>Any relevant insurance requirements from the contract.</li>
    <li>Details of any requested endorsements or policy changes.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>Use the exact legal name and address provided by the requesting party. Incorrect information can result in the certificate being rejected or having to be reissued.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">Step 5: Review the Certificate Before Sending It</h3>

  <p>Do not forward the document without checking it first.</p>

  <p>Confirm that:</p>

  <ul>
    <li>Your business name is correct.</li>
    <li>The correct insurance policies are listed.</li>
    <li>Policy numbers and dates are accurate.</li>
    <li>The stated limits meet the contract requirements.</li>
    <li>The certificate holder's name and address are correct.</li>
    <li>Any required endorsements have actually been issued.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>If something is missing or incorrect, contact your agent or insurer before submitting the document.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">Step 6: Send the COI to the Requesting Party</h3>

  <p>Once the certificate is correct, send it to the client, landlord, contractor or other party that requested it.</p>

  <p>Keep a copy for your business records. If the project continues beyond the policy's expiration date, arrange to provide updated proof of coverage when required.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #123e4e; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;">5. What Is a Certificate Holder?</h2>

  <p>A certificate holder is the person or organization identified on the COI as the party receiving the certificate.</p>

  <p>For example, if a landlord asks your business to provide proof of insurance, the landlord may be named in the certificate holder section.</p>

  <p>This does not automatically make the landlord an insured party under your policy.</p>

  <p>That distinction is important because businesses sometimes confuse a certificate holder with an additional insured.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">Certificate Holder vs Additional Insured</h3>

  <p>A <b>certificate holder</b> receives the certificate as evidence of reported insurance information. The designation alone does not grant coverage under the policy.</p>

  <p>An <b>additional insured</b> is a party that receives certain protection under another party's insurance policy through applicable policy wording or an endorsement.</p>

  <p>For example, a general contractor may require a subcontractor to add the contractor as an additional insured for specified liability arising from the subcontractor's work.</p>

  <p>The insurer must confirm that the required additional insured coverage is supported by the policy or an appropriate endorsement. Simply entering the contractor's name on the COI does not create that protection.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #123e4e; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;">6. What Does an ACORD 25 Certificate of Insurance Show?</h2><h2 style="color: #123e4e; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;"><img alt="ACORD 25® Certificate of Liability Insurance" class="sFlh5c Jm0Uqe FyHeAf uT7N3d" height="640" src="https://www.abbyy.com/marketplace/_next/image/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fmedialib.abbyy.com%2Fv1%2Fmedia%2Fedge%2Fimages%2Fabbyydeveloc5ba-xmc0fa5-prod859e-c28d%2Fmedia%2Fproject%2Fabbyy%2Fmarketplace%2Fassets%2Fdocument-skills%2Fformtran%2Facord-25-certificate-of-liability-insurance%2Fformtran-certificate-of-liability-insurance-sample-3---0001.png%3Fh%3D3300%26iar%3D0%26w%3D2550&amp;w=3840&amp;q=75" style="--xgflle: 0.7727272727272727; max-width: 2550px;" width="495" />&nbsp;</h2>

  <p>The ACORD 25 is a commonly used certificate of liability insurance form. It organizes key insurance information into sections so the requesting party can review the policies and limits reported by the issuing agent or insurer.</p>

  <p>Here are some of the main sections you may encounter.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">Producer Information</h3>

  <p>The producer is generally the insurance agency or broker responsible for arranging the policy and issuing the certificate.</p>

  <p>This section typically includes the agency's name, address and contact information.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">Insured Information</h3>

  <p>This section identifies the business or organization whose insurance policies are being summarized.</p>

  <p>Check that the name matches the legal business name or insured entity shown on the policy.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">Insurer Information</h3>

  <p>This section identifies the insurance companies providing the listed policies.</p>

  <p>It may include insurer names and their National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) identification numbers.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">Types of Insurance and Coverage Limits</h3>

  <p>The coverage section identifies the types of insurance reported on the certificate and shows relevant policy numbers, effective dates, expiration dates and limits.</p>

  <p>Depending on the policies carried, the certificate may show information for:</p>

  <ul>
    <li>Commercial general liability.</li>
    <li>Automobile liability.</li>
    <li>Umbrella or excess liability.</li>
    <li>Workers' compensation and employers' liability.</li>
    <li>Other applicable liability coverage.</li>
  </ul>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">Description of Operations</h3>

  <p>This area may contain information about the project, location or operations associated with the certificate request.</p>

  <p>However, wording in this section cannot independently change the coverage provided by the underlying policy.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">Certificate Holder</h3>

  <p>This section identifies the person or organization receiving the certificate.</p>

  <p>Review the name and address carefully to avoid sending a document that identifies the wrong organization.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #123e4e; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;">7. Does a Certificate of Insurance Cost Money?</h2>

  <p>In many cases, an insurer or broker provides a routine certificate without a separate charge. However, this is not a universal rule, and fees or administrative charges may depend on the provider, service arrangement and jurisdiction.</p>

  <p>More importantly, <b>requesting a COI is different from requesting additional insurance coverage.</b></p>

  <p>If your existing policy already meets the requesting party's requirements, issuing the certificate may simply involve documenting the relevant policy information.</p>

  <p>But if you need to:</p>

  <ul>
    <li>Increase your coverage limits.</li>
    <li>Add a new type of insurance.</li>
    <li>Add an additional insured.</li>
    <li>Arrange a waiver of subrogation.</li>
    <li>Make another policy change.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>Those changes may involve additional premiums, endorsement charges or other costs. The insurer must confirm the actual price and whether the requested change is available.</p>

  <p>Ask your agent to distinguish any certificate-processing fee from the cost of changing your insurance coverage.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #123e4e; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;">8. How Long Does It Take to Get a COI?</h2>

  <p>If your business already has an active policy and the certificate request is straightforward, you may receive the document quickly.</p>

  <p>Some insurers and brokers provide certificates through online portals, allowing customers to download them almost immediately. Other providers process requests through an agent or customer service team.</p>

  <p>Requests involving additional insured endorsements, increased limits, new coverage or underwriting approval may take longer.</p>

  <p>For that reason, do not wait until the day a contract begins to request your certificate. Contact your insurer as soon as you receive the insurance requirements.</p>

  <div style="background: rgb(237, 246, 246); border-radius: 10px; margin: 22px 0px; padding: 18px;">
    <b style="color: #123e4e;">Practical tip</b>
    <p style="margin: 8px 0px 0px;">If a client needs a COI by Friday, send the insurance requirements to your agent several business days earlier. This gives the insurer time to review any requested policy changes before issuing the certificate.</p>
  </div>

  <h2 style="color: #123e4e; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;">9. What If Your COI Does Not Meet the Client's Requirements?</h2>

  <p>Sometimes a certificate is rejected because the coverage does not match what the client, landlord or contractor requested.</p>

  <p>The right solution depends on the specific problem.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">Your Coverage Limits Are Too Low</h3>

  <p>Suppose a client requires $1 million in general liability coverage per occurrence, but your current policy provides only $500,000.</p>

  <p>Contact your insurer and ask whether your limits can be increased. The insurer will review the request and explain any additional premium or underwriting requirements.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">You Do Not Have the Required Type of Insurance</h3>

  <p>A client may require professional liability, commercial auto or workers' compensation coverage that your business does not currently carry.</p>

  <p>In that case, you may need to purchase a separate policy or add an appropriate endorsement if the insurer offers one.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">The Client Requires Additional Insured Status</h3>

  <p>Contact your agent and request the required additional insured endorsement.</p>

  <p>Provide the exact legal name of the organization and any relevant contract wording. The insurer must confirm that the requested endorsement is available and applies to the relevant work.</p>

  <p>Once the change has been properly approved and issued, request an updated certificate.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">The Certificate Contains Incorrect Information</h3>

  <p>If your business name, policy dates, limits or certificate holder details are incorrect, ask the issuing agent or insurer to correct the certificate.</p>

  <p>Do not edit the document yourself. The certificate should be issued by the authorized insurance professional or carrier.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">The Policy Expires Before the Project Ends</h3>

  <p>If the insurance policy expires during a project or lease term, you may need to renew the coverage and provide an updated certificate.</p>

  <p>Make sure the business maintains the required coverage continuously. A certificate showing an old policy term does not prove that coverage remains active after its expiration date.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #123e4e; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;">10. Common COI Mistakes Small Businesses Should Avoid</h2><h2 style="color: #123e4e; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;"><img alt="How Insurance Agents Can Improve Their Marketing to Attract New Business -  Provident" class="sFlh5c Jm0Uqe FyHeAf uT7N3d" height="427" src="https://www.providentins.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/How-Insurance-Agents-Can-Improve-Their-Marketing-to-Attract-New-Business-e1572365777373.jpg" style="--xgflle: 1.5; max-width: 1200px;" width="640" />&nbsp;</h2>

  <p>A certificate may look like a simple administrative document, but mistakes can delay projects or create misunderstandings about insurance protection.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">Mistake 1: Assuming a COI Creates Coverage</h3>

  <p>A certificate summarizes information about insurance policies. It does not independently create, extend or modify coverage.</p>

  <p>The actual insurance policy and applicable endorsements determine the protection available.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">Mistake 2: Confusing the Certificate Holder With an Additional Insured</h3>

  <p>Listing a landlord or client as the certificate holder does not automatically give that party insured status.</p>

  <p>If additional insured protection is required, arrange the appropriate policy provision or endorsement.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">Mistake 3: Ignoring the Contract's Insurance Requirements</h3>

  <p>Do not assume that every client requires the same coverage. One contract may require general liability only, while another may require general liability, professional liability and workers' compensation.</p>

  <p>Review each agreement individually.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">Mistake 4: Sending an Outdated Certificate</h3>

  <p>A COI issued months ago may show a policy that has since expired or changed.</p>

  <p>Request updated documentation when your policy renews or changes, and whenever a client requires current proof.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">Mistake 5: Editing or Creating Your Own Certificate</h3>

  <p>Do not use an image editor, word processor or PDF tool to change a certificate's limits, dates or named parties.</p>

  <p>Only the authorized issuing party should prepare and issue the certificate. Altering insurance documents can misrepresent the coverage your business actually carries.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 20px;">Mistake 6: Waiting Until the Last Minute</h3>

  <p>Routine certificates may be issued quickly, but requests involving policy changes can take longer.</p>

  <p>Send your requirements to your insurer early enough to resolve any coverage gaps before the contract deadline.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #123e4e; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;">12. Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 19px;">Can I get a certificate of insurance without having insurance?</h3>

  <p>No. A COI is evidence of an existing insurance policy. If your business does not have the required coverage, you must obtain an appropriate policy before an insurer or authorized agent can issue a certificate showing that coverage.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 19px;">Can I get a COI online?</h3>

  <p>Yes, some insurers and brokers provide online customer portals where policyholders can request or download certificates. Other providers require you to contact an agent or customer service representative. The available process depends on your insurance provider.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 19px;">Is a certificate of insurance the same as an insurance policy?</h3>

  <p>No. A certificate summarizes reported insurance information, while the insurance policy is the contract that establishes the coverage, conditions, exclusions and obligations. A COI does not replace the policy.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 19px;">Does a COI guarantee that my insurance claim will be covered?</h3>

  <p>No. The certificate does not guarantee payment of a future claim. Coverage depends on the actual policy wording, applicable endorsements, exclusions, conditions and circumstances of the loss.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 19px;">Does a certificate holder have to be an additional insured?</h3>

  <p>No. A certificate holder simply receives the certificate. Additional insured status is a separate insurance arrangement that must be supported by the policy or an appropriate endorsement.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 19px;">Do I need a separate COI for every client?</h3>

  <p>It depends on what each client requests. A separate certificate may be prepared for each requesting party so that its correct name and address appear in the certificate holder section. Ask your insurer how it handles multiple requests.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 19px;">How often should I update my COI?</h3>

  <p>Request an updated certificate when your policy renews, relevant coverage changes, an endorsement is issued or a client asks for current proof. Also review the certificate before beginning a new contract.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 19px;">Can a client require a specific insurance limit?</h3>

  <p>Yes. A client, landlord or contractor can include insurance requirements in its contract, subject to applicable law. If your existing limits do not meet those requirements, contact your insurer to discuss whether they can be increased.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 19px;">Does a COI show all the exclusions in my policy?</h3>

  <p>No. A standard certificate is a summary, not a complete copy of the insurance contract. It does not list every exclusion, condition, limitation or endorsement. Review the actual policy when you need to understand the full scope of coverage.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #187b83; font-size: 19px;">Can an insurance agent change my policy just by updating the COI?</h3>

  <p>No. Changes to coverage must be properly authorized and documented through the insurer's policy-change or endorsement process. Updating the certificate alone does not create additional coverage.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #123e4e; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;">Final Thoughts: Request Your COI Before the Deadline</h2>

  <p>Getting a certificate of insurance is usually a simple process when your business already has the required coverage. Confirm the requesting party's requirements, review your policy, contact your insurer and provide the correct certificate holder details.</p>

  <p>Pay particular attention to additional insured requirements, coverage limits and policy dates. These are common areas where a certificate may fail to satisfy a contract even when a business already has insurance.</p>

  <p>Most importantly, remember that a COI is evidence of reported coverage, not the insurance contract itself. If a client requires additional protection, make sure the underlying policy is properly updated before submitting the certificate.</p>
