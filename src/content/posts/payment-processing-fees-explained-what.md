  <h2 style="color: #123b50; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 35px;">1. What Are Payment Processing Fees?</h2>

  <p>Payment processing fees are charges a business pays to accept and process electronic payments, including credit and debit card transactions.</p>

  <p>When a customer taps a card, inserts a chip, enters card details online or uses a digital wallet, several parties work together to authorize and complete the transaction.</p>

  <p>These may include:</p>

  <ul>
    <li><b>The customer:</b> The person making the purchase.</li>
    <li><b>The issuing bank:</b> The financial institution that issued the customer's payment card.</li>
    <li><b>The card network:</b> The payment network, such as Visa, Mastercard, American Express or Discover.</li>
    <li><b>The acquiring bank:</b> The financial institution that enables the merchant to accept card payments.</li>
    <li><b>The payment processor:</b> The company or service that facilitates payment authorization, processing and settlement.</li>
    <li><b>The merchant:</b> The business receiving the payment.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>Not every provider operates in exactly the same way. Some payment companies combine several roles, while others rely on partner banks and external payment networks.</p>

  <p>The merchant typically sees the processing charges deducted from its transaction proceeds or collected through a separate billing arrangement.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #123b50; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;">2. The Three Main Components of Card Processing Fees</h2>

  <p>One of the most useful things a small business owner can understand is that a card processing fee is not necessarily one charge going to one company.</p>

  <p>In a typical card transaction, the cost can be broken into three main components.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">A. Interchange Fees</h3>

  <p><b>Interchange</b> is a fee associated with card transactions that is generally paid by the merchant's acquiring bank to the cardholder's issuing bank.</p>

  <p>It helps compensate the issuing bank for costs and risks associated with issuing payment cards and handling transactions.</p>

  <p>Interchange rates are influenced by several factors, including:</p>

  <ul>
    <li>The card network.</li>
    <li>Whether the card is credit or debit.</li>
    <li>The card product and category.</li>
    <li>The merchant's business category.</li>
    <li>Whether the payment is made in person or remotely.</li>
    <li>Whether the transaction meets the requirements for a particular interchange category.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>For example, a card-present transaction processed through a properly configured terminal may qualify for a different interchange category from an online transaction where the customer enters card details manually.</p>

  <p>Visa and Mastercard publish interchange schedules for their respective networks. These schedules contain multiple categories and qualification requirements rather than one universal rate.</p>

  <p><b>Important:</b> Interchange is not normally a separate bill that the small business pays directly to the cardholder's bank. It is generally included in the merchant's overall processing cost through its acquiring or processing arrangement.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">B. Card Network Assessment Fees</h3>

  <p>Card networks also charge fees associated with operating and maintaining their payment systems.</p>

  <p>These are often referred to as assessment fees or network fees.</p>

  <p>They are separate from interchange, although both may be incorporated into the total price a processor charges the merchant.</p>

  <p>Network-related costs can vary according to the card network, transaction characteristics and applicable network rules.</p>

  <p>A business using an interchange-plus pricing model may see some network-related charges identified separately on its processing statement. Under a flat-rate arrangement, these underlying costs are generally bundled into the advertised processing rate.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">C. Payment Processor Markup</h3>

  <p>The processor markup is the amount added by the payment processor or acquiring provider for its services.</p>

  <p>Depending on the agreement, it may cover services such as:</p>

  <ul>
    <li>Payment authorization and processing.</li>
    <li>Transaction routing.</li>
    <li>Settlement and reporting.</li>
    <li>Payment technology and infrastructure.</li>
    <li>Customer support.</li>
    <li>Fraud prevention and risk management services.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>The processor markup is particularly important when comparing providers because it is the component that may be negotiated under certain pricing arrangements.</p>

  <p>However, businesses should not assume every advertised fee is negotiable. Network charges, interchange and other third-party costs are governed by their own schedules and agreements.</p>

  <div style="background: rgb(242, 247, 249); border-radius: 10px; border: 1px solid rgb(220, 230, 235); margin: 22px 0px; padding: 19px;">
    <h3 style="color: #123b50; font-size: 18px; margin-top: 0px;">The basic payment processing cost structure</h3>
    <p style="color: #176b87; font-size: 18px; font-weight: bold; margin: 15px 0px; text-align: center;">Interchange + Network Assessments + Processor Markup</p>
    <p style="font-size: 14px; margin: 0px;">This is a simplified description of the underlying card acceptance cost. The way each component appears on a merchant's statement depends on the processor and pricing model.</p>
  </div>

  <h2 style="color: #123b50; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;">3. Common Payment Processing Pricing Models</h2>

  <p>Payment processors do not all package their charges in the same way. Understanding the pricing model is essential because two providers can advertise similar rates while presenting their costs differently.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">Flat-Rate Pricing</h3>

  <p>Flat-rate pricing combines the underlying processing costs and provider markup into a relatively simple rate.</p>

  <p>A provider might charge:</p>

  <p style="background: rgb(242, 247, 249); border-radius: 8px; color: #176b87; font-size: 20px; font-weight: bold; padding: 13px; text-align: center;">2.9% + $0.30 per transaction</p>

  <p>This means the business pays a percentage of the transaction amount plus a fixed charge for each successful payment covered by that rate.</p>

  <p>The actual underlying interchange and network costs may differ between card types, but the merchant pays the advertised blended rate for transactions covered by the provider's pricing terms.</p>

  <p><b>Advantages:</b></p>
  <ul>
    <li>Easy to understand.</li>
    <li>Simple to estimate transaction costs.</li>
    <li>Often convenient for newer or smaller businesses.</li>
    <li>Can reduce the complexity of reviewing processing statements.</li>
  </ul>

  <p><b>Disadvantages:</b></p>
  <ul>
    <li>The business may pay the same advertised rate for card types with different underlying costs.</li>
    <li>It may be less economical for some high-volume merchants.</li>
    <li>Additional fees may still apply to international cards, chargebacks or specialized services.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>Flat-rate pricing is often attractive to small businesses that value predictable costs and straightforward billing.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">Interchange-Plus Pricing</h3>

  <p>Interchange-plus pricing passes the underlying interchange cost through to the merchant and adds a separate processor markup.</p>

  <p>For example, a provider might quote:</p>

  <p style="background: rgb(242, 247, 249); border-radius: 8px; color: #176b87; font-size: 19px; font-weight: bold; padding: 13px; text-align: center;">Interchange + 0.30% + $0.08</p>

  <p>The business pays the applicable interchange and network-related charges, plus the provider's stated markup.</p>

  <p>The exact underlying cost can vary from transaction to transaction. This is why the total processing rate is not necessarily identical for every card payment.</p>

  <p><b>Advantages:</b></p>
  <ul>
    <li>Provides more visibility into underlying processing costs.</li>
    <li>Separates the provider's markup from interchange.</li>
    <li>Can be attractive to businesses with substantial transaction volume.</li>
    <li>Allows merchants to examine how different card types affect costs.</li>
  </ul>

  <p><b>Disadvantages:</b></p>
  <ul>
    <li>Statements can be more complicated.</li>
    <li>The total cost is not always easy to predict before a transaction occurs.</li>
    <li>Businesses may need more time or support to understand the charges.</li>
  </ul>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">Tiered Pricing</h3>

  <p>Tiered pricing groups transactions into categories, often using labels such as qualified, mid-qualified and non-qualified.</p>

  <p>Each category has its own processing rate. The category assigned to a transaction may depend on the card type, payment method, transaction data and the processor's rules.</p>

  <p>The challenge is that the business may not always know in advance which category a transaction will enter.</p>

  <p>A low advertised qualified rate can therefore be misleading if a substantial share of the business's transactions are classified into more expensive categories.</p>

  <p>Before agreeing to tiered pricing, ask the provider for clear definitions of each category and examples of the rates likely to apply to your transactions.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #123b50; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;">4. Why Online and In-Person Payment Fees Can Differ</h2>

  <p>Not all card transactions carry the same processing rate.</p>

  <p>One important distinction is whether the customer presents the physical card or provides payment details remotely.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">Card-Present Transactions&nbsp;</h3>

  <p>Card-present transactions happen when a customer physically uses a card or compatible device at a payment terminal.</p><p><img alt="Payment card Images - Free Download on Magnific (formerly Freepik)" class="sFlh5c Jm0Uqe FyHeAf uT7N3d" height="426" src="https://img.magnific.com/premium-photo/customer-using-credit-card-payment-owner-cafe-restaurant-cashless-technology-credit-card-payment-concept_928174-507.jpg?semt=ais_hybrid&amp;w=740&amp;q=80" style="--xgflle: 1.5010141987829615; max-width: 740px;" width="640" />&nbsp;</p>

  <p>Examples include:</p>

  <ul>
    <li>Inserting a chip card into a terminal.</li>
    <li>Tapping a contactless card.</li>
    <li>Using a supported mobile wallet at a store terminal.</li>
    <li>Swiping a card where that method is supported.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>These transactions can benefit from security features such as chip authentication and contactless payment technology.</p>

  <p>Some processors charge lower standard rates for eligible card-present payments than for online or manually entered card transactions.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">Card-Not-Present Transactions</h3>

  <p>Card-not-present transactions happen when the physical payment card is not presented to the merchant's terminal.</p><p><img alt="Page 4 | Bank card payment Images - Free Download on Magnific (formerly  Freepik)" class="sFlh5c Jm0Uqe FyHeAf uT7N3d" height="426" src="https://img.magnific.com/free-photo/close-up-person-doing-online-shopping_23-2149159936.jpg?semt=ais_hybrid&amp;w=740&amp;q=80" style="--xgflle: 1.5010141987829615; max-width: 740px;" width="640" />&nbsp;</p>

  <p>Common examples include:</p>

  <ul>
    <li>Online store checkouts.</li>
    <li>Payments made through a payment link.</li>
    <li>Card details entered into an invoice payment page.</li>
    <li>Manually entered card payments.</li>
    <li>Some recurring subscription payments.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>These transactions may involve different fraud and verification risks. Consequently, processors may apply different rates or additional fees.</p>

  <p>For example, Stripe's standard US pricing lists 2.9% + $0.30 for successful domestic online card transactions. Its published in-person Terminal rate for domestic cards is 2.7% + $0.05.</p>

  <p>Square's Free plan lists 2.6% + $0.15 for eligible in-person tap, dip or swipe payments and 3.3% + $0.30 for online or invoice payments.</p>

  <p>These are provider-specific examples, not universal industry rates. Different plans, transaction types and eligibility conditions can produce different charges.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #123b50; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;">5. Other Payment Processing Fees Small Businesses Should Know</h2>

  <p>The percentage and fixed charge on each successful payment are not necessarily the only costs a merchant will encounter.</p>

  <p>Here are additional charges worth examining before choosing a payment provider.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">Chargeback and Dispute Fees</h3>

  <p>A chargeback occurs when a cardholder disputes a transaction through the card-issuing bank.</p>

  <p>The customer may claim that a payment was unauthorized, the product was not received, the service was not delivered as promised or another issue occurred.</p>

  <p>When a dispute is opened, the processor may reverse the disputed payment and charge the merchant a dispute-related fee.</p>

  <p>The business may also need to provide evidence, such as:</p>

  <ul>
    <li>Order confirmation records.</li>
    <li>Delivery or tracking information.</li>
    <li>Customer communication.</li>
    <li>Refund and cancellation records.</li>
    <li>Proof of customer authorization.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>Some processors return a dispute response fee if the merchant successfully challenges a dispute, while others have different policies.</p>

  <p>For example, Stripe's US pricing lists a $15 dispute fee. Its dispute documentation explains that a dispute can reverse the payment and result in a separate dispute-related charge.</p>

  <p>Always check the provider's current policy because dispute fees, refund rules and evidence procedures differ.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">International Card Fees</h3>

  <p>Some processors charge additional fees when a business accepts a payment using a card issued in another country.</p>

  <p>This is particularly relevant to online stores serving international customers.</p>

  <p>For example, Stripe lists an additional 1.5% for international cards under its standard US online card pricing.</p>

  <p>Square also lists a 1.5% international transaction fee for payments made using cards issued in a different country from the location of the Square account.</p>

  <p>Do not confuse an international card fee with a currency conversion fee. They may be separate charges.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">Currency Conversion Fees</h3>

  <p>Currency conversion occurs when a payment requires money to be converted from one currency into another.</p>

  <p>For example, a US merchant may sell a product to a customer paying with a card denominated in another currency. If the transaction requires conversion, the processor or another participant in the payment chain may apply a conversion charge.</p>

  <p>Stripe lists an additional 1% when currency conversion is required under its standard US pricing.</p>

  <p>Businesses selling internationally should check whether international card charges and currency conversion charges can apply together.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">Payment Gateway Fees</h3>

  <p>A payment gateway securely captures and transmits payment information between a customer's checkout experience and the payment processing system.</p>

  <p>Some payment providers include gateway functionality in their standard processing service. Others may charge separately for gateway access or additional features.</p>

  <p>Potential gateway-related costs include:</p>

  <ul>
    <li>Monthly gateway subscriptions.</li>
    <li>Gateway transaction charges.</li>
    <li>Advanced fraud screening.</li>
    <li>Recurring billing features.</li>
    <li>Additional integrations or technical services.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>Before paying for a separate gateway, confirm whether your processor already includes the required functionality.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">Monthly, Setup and Account Fees</h3>

  <p>Some merchant service providers charge recurring or one-time fees for access to their payment services.</p>

  <p>These may include:</p>

  <ul>
    <li>Monthly account or service fees.</li>
    <li>Setup or onboarding charges.</li>
    <li>Monthly gateway fees.</li>
    <li>Monthly minimum processing fees.</li>
    <li>Account maintenance charges.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>Not every processor applies these charges. For example, Stripe advertises its standard pay-as-you-go pricing without setup or monthly fees.</p>

  <p>However, a business should examine the specific plan and agreement rather than assuming every optional product or service is included.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">Hardware and Terminal Fees</h3>

  <p>Businesses accepting in-person payments may need card readers, terminals or other point-of-sale equipment.</p>

  <p>Depending on the provider, equipment may be:</p>

  <ul>
    <li>Purchased outright.</li>
    <li>Rented for a monthly fee.</li>
    <li>Included in a software or hardware package.</li>
    <li>Provided under a separate service agreement.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>Also check whether replacement devices, accessories, software subscriptions or technical support involve additional costs.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">PCI Compliance and Security Fees</h3>

  <p>Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) requirements are designed to protect payment card data.</p>

  <p>Businesses that accept card payments must understand which PCI security responsibilities apply to their payment environment.</p>

  <p>Some providers include tools and support that help merchants meet applicable requirements. Others may charge for specific compliance services or impose fees under their agreements.</p>

  <p>Do not assume every processor charges a separate PCI compliance fee. Ask what is included, what the merchant remains responsible for and whether any additional service charge applies.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #123b50; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;">6. How Much Do Payment Processing Fees Cost on a $100 Sale?</h2>

  <p>A simple way to understand processing fees is to calculate the cost of a typical transaction.</p>

  <p>Suppose a customer purchases a $100 product and pays using a domestic card.</p>

  <p>Under Stripe's published US standard pricing:</p>

  <ul>
    <li>Online domestic card rate: 2.9% + $0.30.</li>
    <li>In-person domestic card rate: 2.7% + $0.05.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>For an online transaction:</p>

  <p style="background: rgb(242, 247, 249); border-radius: 8px; color: #176b87; font-weight: bold; padding: 14px; text-align: center;">($100 × 2.9%) + $0.30 = $3.20</p>

  <p>The merchant would receive $96.80 after this processing fee, before any other applicable charges or adjustments.</p>

  <p>For an in-person transaction:</p>

  <p style="background: rgb(242, 247, 249); border-radius: 8px; color: #176b87; font-weight: bold; padding: 14px; text-align: center;">($100 × 2.7%) + $0.05 = $2.75</p>

  <p>The merchant would receive $97.25 after this processing fee, before any other applicable charges or adjustments.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #123b50; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;">7. How to Calculate Your Business's Monthly Processing Costs</h2>

  <p>Estimating the monthly cost of accepting card payments is easier when you separate percentage charges from fixed per-transaction fees.</p>

  <p>For a simple flat-rate pricing arrangement, use this formula:</p>

  <div style="background: rgb(242, 247, 249); border-radius: 10px; border: 1px solid rgb(220, 230, 235); margin: 20px 0px; padding: 20px;">
    <h3 style="color: #123b50; font-size: 18px; margin-top: 0px;">Basic processing cost formula</h3>
    <p style="color: #176b87; font-size: 18px; font-weight: bold; text-align: center;">(Total Card Sales × Percentage Rate) + (Number of Transactions × Fixed Fee)</p>
    <p style="font-size: 14px; margin-bottom: 0px;">Add any applicable monthly, gateway, chargeback, hardware or other service fees separately.</p>
  </div>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">Worked Example: A Small Online Store</h3>

  <p>Imagine an online store processes 300 domestic card transactions in one month.</p>

  <ul>
    <li>Average order value: $50.</li>
    <li>Number of successful transactions: 300.</li>
    <li>Total monthly card sales: $15,000.</li>
    <li>Example processing rate: 2.9% + $0.30.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>Percentage-based charges:</p>
  <p style="background: rgb(242, 247, 249); border-radius: 8px; color: #176b87; font-weight: bold; padding: 12px; text-align: center;">$15,000 × 2.9% = $435</p>

  <p>Fixed transaction charges:</p>
  <p style="background: rgb(242, 247, 249); border-radius: 8px; color: #176b87; font-weight: bold; padding: 12px; text-align: center;">300 × $0.30 = $90</p>

  <p>Total estimated processing fees:</p>
  <p style="background: rgb(234, 245, 248); border-radius: 8px; color: #176b87; font-size: 20px; font-weight: bold; padding: 15px; text-align: center;">$435 + $90 = $525</p>

  <p>Under these assumptions, the store would pay $525 in processing fees for the month, leaving $14,475 from the $15,000 in card sales before other expenses and adjustments.</p>

  <p>This is a simplified example using one flat rate. A business with different card types, international transactions, refunds or multiple payment methods should calculate each category separately.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #123b50; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;">8. How Small Businesses Can Reduce Payment Processing Costs</h2><h2 style="color: #123b50; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;"><img alt="Small business shop Images - Free Download on Magnific (formerly Freepik)" class="sFlh5c Jm0Uqe FyHeAf uT7N3d" height="426" src="https://img.magnific.com/free-photo/cafe-small-business-smiling-asian-barista-apron-standing-counter-coffee-shop-workin_1258-134048.jpg?semt=ais_hybrid&amp;w=740&amp;q=80" style="--xgflle: 1.5010141987829615; max-width: 740px;" width="640" />&nbsp;</h2>

  <p>Payment processing fees are part of accepting electronic payments, but businesses can take practical steps to manage them.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">Compare the Total Cost, Not Just the Advertised Rate</h3>

  <p>One provider may advertise a low percentage rate but charge additional monthly or service fees. Another may have a higher headline rate but include features that would otherwise require separate subscriptions.</p>

  <p>Compare the full cost using your actual transaction volume, average sale value and payment methods.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">Review Your Monthly Processing Statement</h3>

  <p>Review statements for charges you do not recognize, unexpected plan changes, additional service fees and transactions that have been classified differently from what you expected.</p>

  <p>If a charge is unclear, ask the processor to explain its description and how it was calculated.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">Choose the Right Pricing Model</h3>

  <p>A small business with relatively low card volume may value the simplicity of flat-rate pricing.</p>

  <p>A business processing substantial card volume may benefit from requesting an interchange-plus proposal and comparing the processor markup with its current costs.</p>

  <p>There is no universally cheapest pricing model. The right choice depends on the business's payment mix, sales volume, average transaction value and service requirements.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">Encourage Secure, Properly Captured Transactions</h3>

  <p>Where appropriate, use secure chip or contactless terminals for in-person payments and properly configured checkout systems for online transactions.</p>

  <p>Accurate transaction information, secure authentication and good payment practices can help reduce avoidable problems. They do not guarantee a particular interchange rate or eliminate fraud and disputes.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">Reduce Avoidable Chargebacks</h3>

  <p>Clear product descriptions, transparent refund policies, prompt customer support and reliable delivery records can help prevent misunderstandings that lead to payment disputes.</p>

  <p>Businesses should also use suitable fraud screening and keep evidence of legitimate transactions.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">Evaluate ACH and Other Payment Methods</h3>

  <p>For certain business-to-business payments, bank transfers may cost less than card processing.</p>

  <p>For example, an eligible ACH payment may have a different fee structure from a credit card transaction.</p>

  <p>However, compare the transfer limits, settlement times, return rules, customer convenience and protection policies before deciding to replace card payments.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 20px;">Negotiate When Your Processing Volume Justifies It</h3>

  <p>Businesses with substantial processing volume may be able to request custom pricing or volume-based arrangements.</p>

  <p>When negotiating, ask for a clear breakdown of the processor markup, additional charges, contract terms and any minimum monthly requirements.</p>

  <p>Compare proposals using the same transaction assumptions so that you are not comparing different services or fee structures.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #123b50; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;">10. Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 19px;">What is the average payment processing fee for a small business?</h3>

  <p>There is no single rate that applies to every small business. Processing costs depend on the provider, pricing model, payment method, card type, transaction volume and other factors. Some providers publish flat rates, while others use interchange-plus pricing.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 19px;">What is interchange in payment processing?</h3>

  <p>Interchange is a fee generally paid by the merchant's acquiring bank to the bank that issued the customer's card. It is one component of the overall cost of accepting card payments and varies according to transaction and card characteristics.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 19px;">Are interchange fees the same as processing fees?</h3>

  <p>No. Interchange is one component of card processing costs. A merchant's total processing charge may also include network assessments, processor markup and other applicable services or fees.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 19px;">Why are online payment processing fees sometimes higher?</h3>

  <p>Online transactions are generally card-not-present payments and can involve different fraud and verification risks from eligible in-person transactions. Payment providers may therefore apply different rates to online and in-person payments.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 19px;">What is the difference between flat-rate and interchange-plus pricing?</h3>

  <p>Flat-rate pricing combines underlying processing costs and the provider's markup into a blended rate. Interchange-plus pricing passes through applicable interchange costs and adds a separately stated processor markup. Flat-rate pricing is simpler to understand, while interchange-plus can provide more visibility into underlying costs.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 19px;">Do payment processors charge fees on refunds?</h3>

  <p>Refund policies vary by provider. Some processors do not return the original processing fee when a payment is refunded. Others have different rules depending on the transaction, region or pricing arrangement. Check the provider's current refund policy before signing up.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 19px;">What is a chargeback fee?</h3>

  <p>A chargeback or dispute fee is a charge that may apply when a cardholder disputes a payment through the issuing bank. The processor may also reverse the disputed transaction amount. The merchant's ability to challenge the dispute depends on the provider's process and the evidence available.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 19px;">Can a small business negotiate payment processing fees?</h3>

  <p>Some providers offer custom pricing or volume-based arrangements, particularly for businesses with higher transaction volumes. Whether a rate can be negotiated depends on the provider and agreement. Network and interchange charges are not the same as the processor's own markup.</p>

  <h3 style="color: #176b87; font-size: 19px;">Are payment processing fees tax deductible?</h3>

  <p>For US businesses, ordinary and necessary payment processing charges may generally be deductible business expenses, subject to applicable tax rules and the business's circumstances. Keep accurate records and consult a qualified tax professional about your specific situation.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #123b50; font-size: 24px; margin-top: 38px;">Final Thoughts: Understand the Fees Before Choosing a Processor</h2>

  <p>Payment processing fees are more complicated than a single percentage displayed on a pricing page.</p>

  <p>Interchange, card network assessments and processor markup make up the core cost structure, while additional charges may apply to international transactions, chargebacks, payment gateways and other services.</p>

  <p>The best way to control these expenses is to understand your business's payment habits and compare providers using realistic transaction figures.</p>

  <p>Do not automatically choose the processor with the lowest advertised percentage. Consider the complete pricing structure, features, reliability, payout arrangements and support.</p>
