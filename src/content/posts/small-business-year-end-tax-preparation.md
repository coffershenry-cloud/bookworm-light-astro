<h1 style="color: #172554; font-size: 34px; line-height: 1.25; margin-bottom: 18px;">Small Business Year-End Tax Preparation Checklist for 2026</h1>

<p>The end of the year is an important time for every small business owner. Before closing your books for 2026, you need to make sure your income, expenses, payroll information and financial records are properly organized.</p><p style="text-align: center;"><img alt="Desk accounting Photos - Download Free High-Quality Pictures | Magnific  (formerly Freepik)" class="sFlh5c FyHeAf uT7N3d" height="337" src="https://img.magnific.com/premium-photo/tax-forms-receipts-calculator-financial-documents-arranged-desk-with-laptop-ample-copy-space-supporting-tax-filing-accounting-review-expense-calculation-business-documentation_36325-9480.jpg" style="--xgflle: 1.896969696969697; max-width: 626px;" width="640" />&nbsp;</p>

<p>Leaving tax preparation until the last minute can make it harder to identify missing receipts, reconcile business accounts, confirm payments to contractors and prepare accurate financial statements. It can also mean overlooking important tax information that could affect your return.</p>

<p>Fortunately, you do not have to wait until tax season to get organized. A structured year-end review can help you identify outstanding tasks, prepare the documents your tax professional may need and understand your potential tax obligations before filing.</p>

<p>This small business year-end tax preparation checklist covers the main steps to take before December 31, 2026, as well as important tasks to complete in January 2027.</p>

<h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 26px; margin-top: 40px;">1. Review and Confirm All Business Income</h2>

<p>Start by checking whether your business has recorded all income received during 2026.</p>

<p>Income can come from different sources, including direct customer payments, online sales, service fees, cash transactions, payment processors and other business activities. Depending on your business, you may also have income from interest, refunds or the sale of business assets.</p>

<p>Compare your accounting records with your bank statements, sales records, invoices and payment processor reports. This can help you identify deposits that have not been recorded or transactions that have been entered incorrectly.</p>

<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px;">Income records to review</h3>

<ul>
<li>Customer invoices and receipts.</li>
<li>Bank deposits and business bank statements.</li>
<li>Cash sales and point-of-sale reports.</li>
<li>Credit card and online payment processor statements.</li>
<li>Forms 1099 received from clients or payment platforms.</li>
<li>Records of refunds, returned payments and sales adjustments.</li>
<li>Income from the sale of business property, where applicable.</li>
</ul>

<p>Do not assume that income can be ignored simply because you did not receive a Form 1099. Business owners are generally responsible for reporting taxable business income even when a customer or payment platform does not issue an information return.</p>

<h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 26px; margin-top: 40px;">2. Organize and Verify Business Expenses</h2>

<p>Business expenses are an important part of tax preparation because eligible expenses may reduce the income subject to tax.</p>

<p>However, not every payment made by a business is automatically deductible. For a typical Schedule C business, an expense generally needs to be both ordinary and necessary for carrying on the trade or business. Other business structures and tax rules may have additional requirements.</p>

<p>Review your expense records and make sure each transaction has a clear business purpose and supporting documentation.</p>

<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px;">Common expense categories to review</h3>

<ul>
<li><b>Rent and utilities:</b> Business premises, electricity, internet and other eligible operating costs.</li>
<li><b>Office expenses:</b> Supplies, stationery and ordinary office materials.</li>
<li><b>Software and subscriptions:</b> Accounting platforms, business applications and eligible digital services.</li>
<li><b>Advertising and marketing:</b> Eligible promotional campaigns, website expenses and marketing services.</li>
<li><b>Professional services:</b> Accounting, legal, consulting and other qualifying professional fees.</li>
<li><b>Insurance:</b> Eligible business insurance premiums.</li>
<li><b>Travel:</b> Qualifying business travel supported by appropriate records.</li>
<li><b>Vehicle expenses:</b> Eligible business-use vehicle costs, subject to applicable tax rules.</li>
<li><b>Employee expenses:</b> Wages, benefits and other eligible employment costs.</li>
<li><b>Repairs and maintenance:</b> Qualifying costs for maintaining business property and equipment.</li>
</ul>

<p>Keep receipts, invoices, payment confirmations and bank or credit card statements. A transaction description alone may not provide enough information to establish what was purchased and why it was a business expense.</p>

<div style="background: rgb(239, 246, 255); border-left: 4px solid rgb(37, 99, 235); border-radius: 5px; margin: 25px 0px; padding: 18px 20px;">
<p style="color: #172554; margin: 0px;"><b>Practical tip:</b> Separate personal and business transactions wherever possible. Using a dedicated business bank account and business credit card can make year-end reconciliation much easier.</p>
</div>

<h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 26px; margin-top: 40px;">3. Reconcile Your Bank Accounts and Credit Cards</h2>

<p>Reconciliation means comparing the transactions recorded in your accounting system with the statements issued by your financial institutions.</p>

<p>It helps you identify missing transactions, duplicate entries, incorrect amounts and other bookkeeping errors before preparing your financial statements.</p>

<p>For each business account, compare your records with the relevant statement and investigate any differences.</p>

<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px;">Accounts to reconcile</h3>

<ul>
<li>Business checking accounts.</li>
<li>Business savings accounts.</li>
<li>Business credit cards.</li>
<li>Payment processor accounts.</li>
<li>Business loan and financing accounts.</li>
<li>Cash balances and petty cash records.</li>
</ul>

<p>Pay particular attention to outstanding checks, deposits in transit, bank fees, interest charges and transactions that may have been recorded in the wrong accounting category.</p>

<p>If you use accounting software, check that its closing balances agree with your statements. Correcting errors now is generally easier than trying to reconstruct transactions months later.</p>

<h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 26px; margin-top: 40px;">4. Review Business Assets and Equipment Purchases</h2>

<p>Did your business purchase a computer, vehicle, machinery, furniture or other equipment during 2026?</p>

<p>These purchases may need to be treated differently from ordinary operating expenses. Depending on the property, its business use, the date it was placed in service and the applicable tax rules, the cost may be recovered through depreciation or an available expensing provision.</p>

<p>Do not automatically treat every equipment purchase as a fully deductible expense in the year you paid for it.</p>

<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px;">Asset records to collect</h3>

<ul>
<li>Purchase invoices and receipts.</li>
<li>Purchase dates and dates placed in service.</li>
<li>Description and cost of each asset.</li>
<li>Business-use percentage, where relevant.</li>
<li>Financing or loan documentation.</li>
<li>Records of improvements and major repairs.</li>
<li>Prior depreciation deductions.</li>
<li>Records of assets sold, traded in or disposed of.</li>
</ul>

<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px;">Understand depreciation and Section 179</h3>

<p>Depreciation generally allows a business to recover the cost of qualifying property over time. Certain tax provisions may allow eligible businesses to deduct a larger portion of an asset's cost in the year it is placed in service.</p>

<p>For tax years beginning in 2026, the IRS lists a maximum Section 179 expense deduction of $2,560,000. The deduction begins to phase down when the cost of qualifying Section 179 property placed in service during the year exceeds $4,090,000. Special rules and eligibility requirements apply, including limits for certain vehicles.</p>

<p>Additionally, current federal law provides 100% additional first-year depreciation for qualifying property acquired after January 19, 2025, subject to the applicable requirements and elections.</p>

<p>These provisions can have significant tax consequences. Before purchasing equipment primarily for a potential deduction, ask a qualified tax professional whether the asset qualifies and how the deduction would affect your business.</p>

<h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 26px; margin-top: 40px;">5. Review Business Vehicle Expenses and Mileage Records</h2>

<p>If you or your employees use vehicles for business activities, review the related mileage and expense records before the year ends.</p>

<p>Depending on the circumstances and applicable rules, a business may use the standard mileage rate or an actual-expense method. The methods have different requirements, and the choice may affect future deductions.</p>

<li>Total vehicle mileage where required.</li>
<li>Receipts and records for eligible vehicle expenses if using the actual-expense method.</li>
</ul>

<p>Do not include personal commuting as business mileage simply because the trip involves travelling to work. The tax rules distinguish between business transportation and ordinary commuting.</p>

<p>Compare your payroll reports with your accounting records and investigate any differences before preparing year-end forms.</p>

<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px;">Payroll information to review</h3>

<ul>
<li>Employee names and identification information.</li>
<li>Total wages and other taxable compensation.</li>
<li>Federal income tax withholding.</li>
<li>Social Security and Medicare tax information.</li>
<li>Employer payroll tax obligations.</li>
<li>Eligible employee benefits and reimbursements.</li>
<li>Quarterly payroll tax filings and payments.</li>
<li>Forms W-2 and related year-end reporting.</li>

<p>Employers should also review their employment tax filing calendar. For example, Form 941 is generally filed quarterly, with the fourth-quarter return due at the end of January following the calendar year, subject to applicable filing rules and exceptions.</p>

<p>For 2026 Forms W-2, the IRS and Social Security Administration filing deadline is February 1, 2027. Employers should also provide employee copies by the applicable deadline.</p>

<h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 26px; margin-top: 40px;">7. Check Payments to Independent Contractors</h2>

<p>If your business paid freelancers, consultants, subcontractors or other independent service providers during 2026, review those payments before preparing information returns.</p>

<p>Businesses may have to file Form 1099-NEC to report qualifying payments for services made to nonemployees. The reporting requirement depends on the nature of the payment, the recipient and the applicable rules.</p>

<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px;">What to check</h3>

<ul>
<li>Names and business details of contractors.</li>
<li>Completed Forms W-9 where required.</li>
<li>Total payments made to each contractor.</li>
<li>Payment dates and transaction records.</li>
<li>Whether payments were made through a payment card or third-party network that has separate reporting responsibilities.</li>
<li>Whether the payments meet the applicable Form 1099 reporting requirements.</li>
</ul>

<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px;">Important 2026 Form 1099-NEC threshold change</h3>

<p>For qualifying payments made during 2026, the general Form 1099-NEC reporting threshold increased to $2,000. The previous $600 threshold applies to payments made before 2026.</p>

<p>This is an important change for businesses preparing their 2026 contractor records. However, the threshold is not the only factor that determines whether a payment must be reported. Certain payments, including payments subject to backup withholding, may still require reporting regardless of the amount.</p>

<p>The general deadline for providing Form 1099-NEC to recipients and filing it with the IRS is January 31. Because January 31, 2027 falls on a Sunday, the next business day is February 1, 2027.</p>

<p>Confirm the filing method and any applicable exceptions with the IRS before submitting forms.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/am-i-required-to-file-a-form-1099-or-other-information-return" rel="noopener" style="color: #2563eb;" target="_blank">Read the IRS Form 1099 reporting requirements</a></p>

<h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 26px; margin-top: 40px;">8. Review Inventory and Cost of Goods Sold</h2>

<p>If your business sells physical products, year-end inventory may be an important part of your tax preparation.</p>

<p>Inventory records help determine the cost of goods sold and the value of products remaining at the end of the accounting period. Incorrect inventory figures can affect the business's reported profit.</p>

<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px;">Inventory tasks to complete</h3>

<ul>
<li>Count physical inventory where required by your accounting method.</li>
<li>Compare actual stock with inventory records.</li>
<li>Identify damaged, obsolete or unsellable goods.</li>
<li>Review inventory purchases and supplier invoices.</li>
<li>Record relevant inventory adjustments.</li>
<li>Confirm that the cost of goods sold calculation is consistent with your accounting method.</li>
</ul>

<p>Do not automatically write off unsold or damaged inventory without checking the applicable tax and accounting rules. The correct treatment depends on the business's circumstances and accounting method.</p>

<h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 26px; margin-top: 40px;">9. Review Estimated Tax Payments</h2>

<p>Many business owners need to make estimated tax payments throughout the year rather than waiting until they file their annual tax return.</p>

<p>This is particularly relevant to self-employed individuals, sole proprietors and some owners of pass-through businesses who do not have enough tax withheld from other income sources.</p>

<p>Review the estimated tax payments you made during 2026 and compare them with your expected tax obligation. If you have not paid enough, you may need to make an additional payment or discuss the situation with a tax professional.</p>

<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px;">The final 2026 estimated tax payment</h3>

<p>For calendar-year taxpayers who are required to make estimated tax payments, the fourth installment for 2026 is generally due on January 15, 2027.</p>

<p>The IRS provides an exception for certain taxpayers who file their 2026 individual income tax return and pay the full balance by the applicable January deadline. Eligibility depends on the circumstances, so do not assume that the January estimated payment can always be skipped.</p>

<p>Estimated tax payments are not necessarily the same for every business structure. Corporations, for example, generally follow different estimated tax rules.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.irs.gov/publications/p505" rel="noopener" style="color: #2563eb;" target="_blank">Read IRS Publication 505: Tax Withholding and Estimated Tax</a></p>

<h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 26px; margin-top: 40px;">10. Review Potential Business Tax Deductions and Credits</h2>

<p>Before closing your books, review whether your business may qualify for deductions or tax credits that you have not yet considered.</p>

<p>This does not mean making purchases simply to create deductions. Instead, the goal is to make sure you have correctly recorded eligible business costs and identified tax provisions that may apply to your activities.</p>

<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px;">Areas worth reviewing</h3>

<ul>
<li><b>Business operating expenses:</b> Confirm that qualifying ordinary and necessary expenses have been recorded.</li>
<li><b>Depreciation:</b> Review eligible equipment, machinery and other business property.</li>
<li><b>Section 179:</b> Determine whether qualifying property may be eligible for expensing.</li>
<li><b>Qualified business income deduction:</b> Eligible taxpayers may qualify for a deduction under Section 199A, subject to income thresholds, business-type rules and other requirements.</li>
<li><b>Retirement plan contributions:</b> Review applicable contribution rules and deadlines for your business and employees.</li>
<li><b>Research and development:</b> Businesses conducting qualifying research activities may have relevant deductions or credits to review.</li>
<li><b>Employer-related credits:</b> Check whether your business qualifies for any available employment-related tax credits.</li>
</ul>

<p>Tax deductions and credits have different eligibility rules. A tax professional can help determine whether a particular provision applies to your business and whether an election or supporting documentation is required.</p>

<h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 26px; margin-top: 40px;">11. Prepare and Review Your Financial Statements</h2>

<p>Once income, expenses, accounts and other records have been reviewed, prepare your year-end financial statements.</p>

<p>These statements help summarize the business's financial position and provide useful information for tax preparation, planning and decision-making.</p>

<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px;">The main financial statements to review</h3>

<p><b>Profit and loss statement:</b> Summarizes business income, expenses and profit or loss over a specific period.</p>

<p><b>Balance sheet:</b> Shows the business's assets, liabilities and equity at a particular date.</p>

<p><b>Cash flow statement:</b> Helps explain how cash moved into and out of the business during the reporting period.</p>

<p>Check that the figures in these reports agree with your underlying accounting records. If something looks unusual, investigate it before using the statements for tax preparation.</p>

<h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 26px; margin-top: 40px;">12. Organize Your Tax Documents and Supporting Records</h2>

<p>Good recordkeeping is essential when preparing a business tax return. Your records should support the income, expenses, deductions and credits reported on that return.</p>

<p>The IRS does not require every business to use the same bookkeeping system. However, the system must clearly show business income and expenses and provide records that support the transactions reported.</p>

<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px;">Documents to gather</h3>

<ul>
<li>Business bank statements.</li>
<li>Business credit card statements.</li>
<li>Sales invoices and customer receipts.</li>
<li>Purchase invoices and expense receipts.</li>
<li>Payroll reports and employment tax records.</li>
<li>Forms W-2, 1099 and other relevant information returns.</li>
<li>Loan statements and interest records.</li>
<li>Equipment and asset purchase documents.</li>
<li>Vehicle mileage logs and supporting records.</li>
<li>Inventory reports, where applicable.</li>
<li>Prior-year tax returns.</li>
<li>Records of estimated tax payments.</li>
<li>Relevant business licenses and registration information.</li>
</ul>

<p>Keep documents organized by year and category. Electronic records can be useful, but they should remain accessible, accurate and adequately protected.</p>

<p>The IRS also explains that employment tax records generally need to be retained for at least four years. Other records may need to be kept for different periods depending on the transaction and the tax issue involved.</p>

<h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 26px; margin-top: 40px;">Common Year-End Tax Preparation Mistakes to Avoid</h2>

<p>Even businesses with accounting software can make mistakes when preparing their tax records. Watch out for these common problems:</p>

<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px;">Mixing Personal and Business Expenses</h3>

<p>Using business accounts for personal purchases can make it harder to identify deductible expenses. Keep personal and business transactions separate and record any owner withdrawals or contributions correctly.</p>

<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px;">Forgetting Small Expenses</h3>

<p>Small purchases can add up over a year. Review receipts, bank statements and credit card statements instead of relying entirely on memory.</p>

<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px;">Treating Every Purchase as a Deduction</h3>

<p>Some purchases may need to be capitalized and depreciated rather than deducted immediately. Review major equipment and property purchases carefully.</p>

<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px;">Ignoring Contractor Reporting Requirements</h3>

<p>Businesses that pay independent contractors should review their information-return obligations and collect the necessary details early. Do not wait until the final filing deadline to discover that contractor information is missing.</p>

<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px;">Using Incorrect Mileage Rates</h3>

<p>The 2026 business mileage rate changed on July 1. Businesses using the standard mileage method should distinguish between miles driven during the first and second halves of the year.</p>

<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 21px;">Waiting Until Tax Season to Find Missing Records</h3>

<p>Trying to reconstruct an entire year's transactions in January or February can be stressful and time-consuming. Review your records before closing the year and resolve missing information as early as possible.</p>

<h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 26px; margin-top: 40px;">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<div style="border-radius: 6px; border: 1px solid rgb(219, 226, 234); margin: 15px 0px; padding: 17px;">
<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 19px; margin-top: 0px;">What should a small business do before the end of the tax year?</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom: 0px;">A small business should review income and expenses, reconcile bank and credit card accounts, organize receipts, check payroll records, review contractor payments, verify asset purchases and prepare year-end financial statements. It should also review estimated tax payments and identify any outstanding reporting responsibilities.</p>
</div>

<div style="border-radius: 6px; border: 1px solid rgb(219, 226, 234); margin: 15px 0px; padding: 17px;">
<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 19px; margin-top: 0px;">What documents do I need to prepare my small business taxes?</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom: 0px;">Common documents include bank statements, credit card statements, invoices, receipts, payroll reports, contractor payment records, loan statements, asset purchase documents, mileage logs and prior-year tax returns. The exact documents depend on your business structure and activities.</p>
</div>

<div style="border-radius: 6px; border: 1px solid rgb(219, 226, 234); margin: 15px 0px; padding: 17px;">
<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 19px; margin-top: 0px;">When should a small business start preparing its taxes?</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom: 0px;">It is best to review financial records throughout the year and begin a detailed year-end review before December 31. January can then be used to complete remaining reconciliations, gather information returns and prepare documents for the tax return.</p>
</div>

<div style="border-radius: 6px; border: 1px solid rgb(219, 226, 234); margin: 15px 0px; padding: 17px;">
<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 19px; margin-top: 0px;">What is the 2026 business mileage rate?</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom: 0px;">The IRS optional business mileage rate is 72.5 cents per mile from January 1 through June 30, 2026, and 76 cents per mile from July 1 through December 31, 2026. Eligibility and recordkeeping requirements still apply.</p>
</div>

<div style="border-radius: 6px; border: 1px solid rgb(219, 226, 234); margin: 15px 0px; padding: 17px;">
<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 19px; margin-top: 0px;">What is the 2026 Form 1099-NEC reporting threshold?</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom: 0px;">For qualifying payments made during 2026, the general reporting threshold increased to $2,000. Certain exceptions apply, so businesses should review the IRS instructions rather than relying on the threshold alone.</p>
</div>

<div style="border-radius: 6px; border: 1px solid rgb(219, 226, 234); margin: 15px 0px; padding: 17px;">
<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 19px; margin-top: 0px;">When is the final estimated tax payment for 2026 due?</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom: 0px;">The fourth estimated tax installment for calendar-year 2026 is generally due January 15, 2027. Some taxpayers may qualify for an exception if they file their return and pay the full balance by the applicable January deadline.</p>
</div>

<div style="border-radius: 6px; border: 1px solid rgb(219, 226, 234); margin: 15px 0px; padding: 17px;">
<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 19px; margin-top: 0px;">How long should a small business keep tax records?</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom: 0px;">The retention period depends on the type of record and the transaction involved. Employment tax records generally must be kept for at least four years. Other business records should be retained for as long as necessary to support the income, deductions and other items reported on the relevant tax returns.</p>
</div>

<div style="border-radius: 6px; border: 1px solid rgb(219, 226, 234); margin: 15px 0px; padding: 17px;">
<h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 19px; margin-top: 0px;">Can accounting software prepare my business tax return automatically?</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom: 0px;">Some accounting platforms provide tax-related reports or integrations with tax preparation products. However, accounting software does not necessarily prepare or file every business tax return. You should confirm what the software supports and whether you need a qualified tax professional.</p>
</div>

<h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 26px; margin-top: 40px;">Final Thoughts: Get Your Business Tax Records Ready Before 2027</h2>

<p>Year-end tax preparation does not have to become a last-minute scramble. By reviewing your financial records before December 31, you can identify missing information, correct bookkeeping errors and prepare for the reporting responsibilities that follow in January.</p>

<p>Start with the basics: confirm your income, organize your expenses, reconcile your accounts and check your payroll and contractor records. Then review equipment purchases, mileage, inventory, estimated taxes and any deductions or credits that may apply to your business.</p>

<p>Most importantly, keep documentation to support the figures you report. Accurate records make tax preparation more manageable and give you a clearer picture of how your business performed during the year.</p>
