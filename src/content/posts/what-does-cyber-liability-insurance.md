  <h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 27px; margin-top: 38px;">What Is Cyber Liability Insurance?</h2>

  <p>
    Cyber liability insurance is a type of business insurance designed to help protect a company against certain financial losses and legal liabilities resulting from cyber incidents.
  </p>

  <p>
    These incidents can include hacking, ransomware, phishing attacks, data breaches, unauthorised access to computer systems and other covered security or privacy events.
  </p>

  <p>
    Imagine a small online retailer that stores customer names, email addresses and payment-related information. An attacker gains access to its systems and exposes customer records. The retailer may need cybersecurity specialists to investigate the incident, lawyers to advise on its obligations, and a service provider to notify affected customers.
  </p>

  <p>
    If the incident also prevents the retailer from processing orders, it may lose income while systems are restored. A suitable cyber insurance policy could help cover some of these expenses, subject to its terms.
  </p>

  <p>
    Cyber insurance does not prevent attacks or replace good cybersecurity practices. It is a financial risk-management tool that can complement security controls such as multifactor authentication, employee training, backups and software updates.
  </p>

  <h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 27px; margin-top: 38px;">Why Do Small Businesses Need Cyber Insurance?</h2>

  <p>
    Small businesses often assume cybercriminals are primarily interested in large corporations. In reality, smaller organisations can also face cyber risks because they use email, online payment systems, cloud applications, websites and digital customer records.
  </p>

  <p>
    A business does not need a large technology department to experience a serious incident. A fraudulent email, compromised password or vulnerable software application can create problems for a company of almost any size.
  </p>

  <p>Cyber insurance may be worth considering if your business:</p>

  <ul style="padding-left: 24px;">
    <li>Stores customer names, addresses, financial details or other personal information.</li>
    <li>Accepts credit card or other digital payments.</li>
    <li>Uses cloud-based software to manage important business operations.</li>
    <li>Maintains an online store, customer portal or booking system.</li>
    <li>Stores employee records or confidential business information.</li>
    <li>Provides professional services involving sensitive client information.</li>
    <li>Depends on computer systems to process orders, communicate with customers or deliver services.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    The potential impact of an incident depends on the information a business holds, its technology environment, its operations and the applicable legal requirements. Cyber insurance can help manage certain financial consequences, but it should be considered alongside a broader risk-management plan.
  </p>

  <h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 27px; margin-top: 40px;">What Does Cyber Liability Insurance Cover?</h2>

  <p>
    Coverage varies between insurers and policies. However, many cyber insurance products address several common categories of loss.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 22px;">1. Data Breach Response Expenses</h3>

  <p>
    A data breach occurs when sensitive information is accessed, disclosed, stolen or exposed without authorisation.
  </p>

  <p>
    For example, hackers might access a business database containing customer details. The company may need to investigate how the attackers entered the system, identify which records were affected and determine what steps are required to respond.
  </p>

  <p>Depending on the policy, data breach response coverage may help pay for:</p>

  <ul style="padding-left: 24px;">
    <li>Digital forensic investigations.</li>
    <li>Legal advice about notification obligations.</li>
    <li>Customer notification services.</li>
    <li>Call centre support for affected individuals.</li>
    <li>Credit monitoring or identity protection services.</li>
    <li>Public relations and crisis communication support.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    These services can be particularly important for businesses that handle personal, financial or confidential customer information.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 22px;">2. Ransomware and Cyber Extortion</h3>

  <p>
    Ransomware is a type of malicious software that can encrypt a business's files or prevent access to important systems. Attackers may demand payment in exchange for restoring access or promise not to publish stolen information.
  </p>

  <p>
    A cyber extortion policy may help cover eligible expenses associated with responding to such threats. Depending on the wording, this could include specialist negotiations, forensic investigations, legal advice and ransom payments where legally permitted and covered.
  </p>

  <p>
    Do not assume every policy automatically pays a ransom. Some policies require the insurer's prior written consent, impose specific conditions or exclude certain payments. Legal restrictions may also affect whether a payment can be made.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 22px;">3. Business Interruption Losses</h3>

  <p>
    A cyber incident can prevent a business from operating normally. An online store may become unavailable, a payment system may stop working or employees may lose access to essential files.
  </p>

  <p>
    Business interruption coverage may help compensate for eligible lost income and additional operating expenses during a covered disruption.
  </p>

  <p>
    For example, if a ransomware attack forces an online retailer to suspend sales while its systems are restored, the policy may help cover certain lost income during the covered interruption period.
  </p>

  <p>
    Pay attention to the policy's waiting period, indemnity period, calculation method and requirements for demonstrating lost income. Some policies also distinguish between direct system failures and interruptions caused by an external service provider.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 22px;">4. Legal Defence and Privacy Liability</h3>

  <p>
    When customer or employee information is exposed, affected individuals may bring claims against the business. Regulators may also investigate whether the company complied with applicable privacy and data protection obligations.
  </p>

  <p>
    Third-party cyber liability coverage may help with eligible legal defence expenses, settlements and certain regulatory response costs.
  </p>

  <p>
    Coverage for regulatory fines and penalties is not universal. It depends on the policy, the nature of the loss and whether the relevant payment is legally insurable.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 22px;">5. Customer Notification and Credit Monitoring</h3>

  <p>
    Businesses may have to notify affected customers after certain data breaches. Depending on the incident and applicable law, they may also need to provide support services to people whose information has been exposed.
  </p>

  <p>
    A cyber policy may help cover eligible notification expenses, call centre services, credit monitoring and identity protection services.
  </p>

  <p>
    These costs can become significant when a business has a large customer database or handles particularly sensitive information.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 22px;">6. Data and System Restoration</h3>

  <p>
    Cyberattacks can damage, encrypt, delete or corrupt business data. Restoring systems may require specialist technicians, backup recovery, software reinstallation and other technical work.
  </p>

  <p>
    Depending on the policy, coverage may help pay for recovering or restoring lost digital information and getting affected systems operational again.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 22px;">7. Cybercrime and Fraudulent Funds Transfer</h3>

  <p>
    Some cyber insurance products include optional or separate protection against certain cybercrime losses.
  </p>

  <p>
    One example is social engineering fraud, where an attacker impersonates a supplier, executive or business partner and tricks an employee into transferring money to an unauthorised account.
  </p>

  <p>
    Not every cyber liability policy covers this type of financial loss automatically. Check whether funds transfer fraud, invoice manipulation and social engineering are included, and whether separate limits or conditions apply.
  </p>

  <h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 27px; margin-top: 40px;">First-Party vs Third-Party Cyber Insurance Coverage</h2>

  <p>
    One of the most useful distinctions when comparing cyber policies is the difference between first-party and third-party coverage.
  </p>

  <p>
    <b>First-party coverage</b> addresses certain direct costs and financial losses experienced by your own business after a covered cyber incident.
  </p>

  <p>
    <b>Third-party coverage</b> addresses certain claims made against your business by customers, clients, business partners or other affected parties.
  </p>

  <h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 27px; margin-top: 40px;">What Does Cyber Liability Insurance Not Cover?</h2>

  <p>
    Cyber insurance does not cover every financial loss involving computers, networks or digital information. Exclusions differ between policies, so the actual contract should always be reviewed.&nbsp;</p><p style="text-align: center;"><img alt="What is a cyber attack? Recent examples show disturbing trends | CSO Online" class="sFlh5c FyHeAf uT7N3d" height="426" src="https://www.csoonline.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/cso_cybersecurity_cyber_attack_warning_danger_threat_hack_by_matejmo_gettyimages-486818926_2400x1600-100813827-orig-1.jpg?quality=50&amp;strip=all&amp;w=1024" style="--xgflle: 1.501466275659824; max-width: 1024px;" width="640" />&nbsp;</p>

  <p>Common exclusions or limitations may include:</p>

  <ul style="padding-left: 24px;">
    <li><b>Known incidents:</b> Events that occurred or were known before the policy began may not be covered.</li>
    <li><b>Intentional wrongdoing:</b> Fraudulent or deliberately criminal acts by the insured may be excluded.</li>
    <li><b>War and certain state-sponsored attacks:</b> Policies may contain exclusions for specified war-related or state-backed cyber operations.</li>
    <li><b>Physical property damage:</b> Damage to buildings, equipment or other physical property may require separate coverage or a specific endorsement.</li>
    <li><b>Uncovered financial fraud:</b> Certain fraudulent transfers or social engineering losses may require additional protection.</li>
    <li><b>Third-party service failures:</b> A cloud provider or vendor outage may be excluded unless dependent business interruption coverage applies.</li>
    <li><b>Failure to meet security conditions:</b> A claim may be affected if the business fails to maintain security measures required by the policy.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    For example, a business that experiences a cloud service outage may discover that its policy covers interruptions caused by attacks on its own systems but not outages at an external provider. The distinction can make a significant difference.
  </p>

  <div style="background: repeat rgb(255, 247, 237); border-left-color: rgb(234, 88, 12); border-left-style: solid; border-left-width: 5px; border-left: 5px solid rgb(234, 88, 12); border-radius: 6px; margin: 28px 0px; padding: 18px 22px;">
    <b style="color: #9a3412;">Important policy reminder</b>
    <p style="margin: 8px 0px 0px;">
      Never assume that a type of incident is covered simply because it appears in an insurer's marketing description. Check the policy definitions, exclusions, endorsements, sublimits, waiting periods and claim conditions before purchasing.
    </p>
  </div>

  </p>

  <p>

  <h3 style="color: #1d4ed8; font-size: 22px;">What Factors Affect Cyber Insurance Premiums?</h3>

  <p>
    Several factors can influence how much a business pays.
  </p>

  <p><b>1. Business size and annual revenue</b></p>
  <p>
    Larger businesses may have more employees, systems and customer records to protect. Revenue can also influence the potential financial impact of a business interruption.
  </p>

  <p><b>2. Industry and type of business</b></p>
  <p>
    Businesses handling medical, financial or other sensitive information may have different exposure from companies that collect very little personal data. Retailers and online businesses may also face risks associated with payment systems and customer accounts.
  </p>

  <p><b>3. Amount and sensitivity of stored data</b></p>
  <p>
    The type and volume of information a business stores can influence its potential breach costs. A company holding extensive personal or financial records may need different limits from a business with fewer sensitive records.
  </p>

  <p><b>4. Cybersecurity practices</b></p>
  <p>
    Insurers may ask about multifactor authentication, backups, endpoint protection, employee training, access controls and incident response procedures. Stronger security practices can improve the business's risk profile, although they do not guarantee a lower premium.
  </p>

  <p><b>5. Coverage limits and deductibles</b></p>
  <p>
    A higher coverage limit generally means the insurer is accepting responsibility for a larger potential loss. The deductible or retention is the amount the business must pay before certain coverage responds. Both can affect the price.
  </p>

  <p><b>6. Claims history</b></p>
  <p>
    Previous cyber incidents or insurance claims may influence underwriting decisions and pricing.
  </p>

  <p><b>7. Business location and operations</b></p>
  <p>
    The states where a company operates, the information it handles and the services it provides can affect the risks and requirements considered by an insurer.
  </p>

  <h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 27px; margin-top: 40px;">What Cybersecurity Requirements Do Insurers Expect?</h2>

  <p>
    Cyber insurance is not a substitute for cybersecurity. Insurers commonly ask questions about how a business protects its systems and information before offering coverage.
  </p>

  <p>
    Requirements vary, but businesses may be asked whether they have implemented:
  </p>

  <ul style="padding-left: 24px;">
    <li><b>Multifactor authentication (MFA):</b> An additional verification step beyond a password, particularly for remote access, email and administrator accounts.</li>
    <li><b>Regular backups:</b> Copies of important data that can be restored after accidental deletion, system failure or ransomware.</li>
    <li><b>Endpoint protection:</b> Security software and controls for laptops, desktops, servers and other connected devices.</li>
    <li><b>Software updates:</b> A process for installing security patches and addressing known vulnerabilities.</li>
    <li><b>Access controls:</b> Limits on who can view, change or manage sensitive information.</li>
    <li><b>Employee training:</b> Guidance to help staff identify phishing messages, suspicious links and social engineering attempts.</li>
    <li><b>Incident response planning:</b> A documented process for reporting, investigating and responding to cyber incidents.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    Some insurers may also ask about encryption, remote access, payment card security, third-party vendors and previous security incidents.
  </p>

  <p>
    The <a href="https://content.naic.org/resource-center/2026-report-cybersecurity-insurance-market" rel="noopener noreferrer" style="color: #2563eb;" target="_blank">National Association of Insurance Commissioners' 2026 cyber insurance market report</a> discusses how security practices such as employee training, access controls, updates, backups and vendor management influence cyber risk.
  </p>

  <h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 27px; margin-top: 40px;">How to Choose the Right Cyber Insurance Policy</h2>

  <p>
    The best policy is not necessarily the one with the lowest advertised premium. It is the policy that addresses the risks your business actually faces and provides suitable limits, conditions and response support.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #334155; font-size: 21px;">1. Identify the Information Your Business Holds</h3>

  <p>
    Start by identifying the information your company collects, stores and processes. This may include customer contact details, payment information, employee records, financial documents and confidential client files.
  </p>

  <p>
    The type and quantity of information you hold can help determine which coverage features matter most.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #334155; font-size: 21px;">2. Understand Your Dependence on Technology</h3>

  <p>
    Consider what would happen if your website, payment system, email platform or cloud software became unavailable for several days.
  </p>

  <p>
    If a disruption would prevent your business from operating or generating revenue, business interruption coverage deserves particular attention.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #334155; font-size: 21px;">3. Compare First-Party and Third-Party Protection</h3>

  <p>
    Check whether the policy includes both direct incident response expenses and protection against claims from customers or other affected parties.
  </p>

  <p>
    A business that handles confidential customer information may need both categories to address different consequences of the same incident.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #334155; font-size: 21px;">4. Review Coverage Limits and Sublimits</h3>

  <p>
    A policy may have an overall coverage limit as well as smaller limits for specific expenses, such as ransomware, social engineering or notification costs.
  </p>

  <p>
    Make sure these limits are appropriate for the potential losses your business could face. A high overall limit does not necessarily mean every coverage category has the same limit.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #334155; font-size: 21px;">5. Examine Exclusions and Waiting Periods</h3>

  <p>
    Read the exclusions carefully. Pay particular attention to prior incidents, third-party service outages, social engineering, unpatched systems, security requirements and war-related exclusions.
  </p>

  <p>
    For business interruption coverage, check whether a waiting period applies before the policy begins paying eligible losses.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #334155; font-size: 21px;">6. Check the Insurer's Incident Response Support</h3>

  <p>
    When an attack happens, a business may need immediate help from forensic specialists, lawyers, breach notification providers and recovery professionals.
  </p>

  <p>
    Ask whether the insurer provides a 24-hour incident hotline, which response providers are available and whether you must obtain approval before hiring specialists.
  </p>

  <h3 style="color: #334155; font-size: 21px;">7. Compare the Full Cost</h3>

  <p>
    Compare premiums, deductibles, retentions, policy limits and additional charges. A cheaper policy may offer lower limits or exclude an important risk.
  </p>

  <p>
    Request quotes using the same coverage limits and business information where possible. This makes it easier to compare the actual value of different offers.
  </p>

  <h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 27px; margin-top: 40px;">What Information Will Insurers Ask For?</h2>

  <p>
    When applying for cyber insurance, businesses may need to complete an application describing their operations, technology environment and security practices.&nbsp;</p><p style="text-align: center;"><img alt="7 Ways to be More Productive as a Small Business Owner | Delegated  (formerly Red Butler)" class="sFlh5c FyHeAf uT7N3d" height="427" src="https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/5dc393eb429c1ad690f7818d/5ebeb5f3c4060e6179e914b1_small-business-owner-indian-man.jpg" style="--xgflle: 1.4992503748125936; max-width: 1000px;" width="640" />&nbsp;</p>

  <p>Depending on the insurer, you may be asked for:</p>

  <ul style="padding-left: 24px;">
    <li>Business name, address, industry and annual revenue.</li>
    <li>Number of employees and business locations.</li>
    <li>Types of customer and employee information stored.</li>
    <li>Details of websites, online stores and payment systems.</li>
    <li>Cloud services and important third-party technology providers.</li>
    <li>Use of multifactor authentication and endpoint protection.</li>
    <li>Backup procedures and recovery arrangements.</li>
    <li>Previous cyber incidents, insurance claims or security breaches.</li>
    <li>Requested coverage limits and deductible amounts.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    Answer application questions accurately. Incomplete or incorrect information can create problems during underwriting and may affect coverage or a future claim.
  </p>

  <h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 27px; margin-top: 40px;">How to Buy Cyber Liability Insurance</h2>

  <ol style="padding-left: 24px;">
    <li><b>Assess your cyber risks.</b> Identify the data you hold, systems you rely on and potential financial consequences of an attack.</li>
    <li><b>Decide what coverage you need.</b> Consider data breach response, ransomware, business interruption, privacy liability and cybercrime protection.</li>
    <li><b>Prepare your business information.</b> Gather revenue details, employee information and descriptions of your cybersecurity controls.</li>
    <li><b>Request quotes.</b> Contact insurers or a licensed insurance broker and provide accurate information.</li>
    <li><b>Compare policy documents.</b> Review limits, exclusions, deductibles, waiting periods and incident response services.</li>
    <li><b>Ask questions before purchasing.</b> Clarify anything that is unclear, particularly optional coverage and conditions that could affect a claim.</li>
    <li><b>Maintain your security practices.</b> Continue following the cybersecurity requirements and policy conditions after purchasing coverage.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 27px; margin-top: 42px;">Frequently Asked Questions About Cyber Liability Insurance</h2>

  <div style="border-bottom-color: rgb(226, 232, 240); border-bottom-style: solid; border-bottom-width: 1px; border-bottom: 1px solid rgb(226, 232, 240); padding: 17px 0px;">
    <h3 style="color: #172554; font-size: 19px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">Is cyber liability insurance worth it for a small business?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">
      It may be worthwhile if your business stores sensitive information, accepts digital payments or relies heavily on technology. The value depends on your potential exposure, the cost of coverage and the protection offered by the policy.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div style="border-bottom-color: rgb(226, 232, 240); border-bottom-style: solid; border-bottom-width: 1px; border-bottom: 1px solid rgb(226, 232, 240); padding: 17px 0px;">
    <h3 style="color: #172554; font-size: 19px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">Does cyber insurance cover ransomware attacks?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">
      Many cyber policies offer some protection for covered ransomware and cyber extortion incidents. However, ransom payments, recovery expenses and business interruption losses are subject to policy conditions, exclusions and legal restrictions.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div style="border-bottom-color: rgb(226, 232, 240); border-bottom-style: solid; border-bottom-width: 1px; border-bottom: 1px solid rgb(226, 232, 240); padding: 17px 0px;">
    <h3 style="color: #172554; font-size: 19px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">Does cyber insurance cover phishing attacks?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">
      It may cover certain losses arising from phishing, including data breach response and some cybercrime expenses. However, fraudulent money transfers and social engineering losses may require specific coverage or an optional endorsement.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div style="border-bottom-color: rgb(226, 232, 240); border-bottom-style: solid; border-bottom-width: 1px; border-bottom: 1px solid rgb(226, 232, 240); padding: 17px 0px;">
    <h3 style="color: #172554; font-size: 19px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">Does cyber insurance cover lost business income?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">
      Some policies include business interruption coverage for eligible losses caused by covered cyber incidents. Waiting periods, indemnity periods and conditions relating to the affected systems may apply.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div style="border-bottom-color: rgb(226, 232, 240); border-bottom-style: solid; border-bottom-width: 1px; border-bottom: 1px solid rgb(226, 232, 240); padding: 17px 0px;">
    <h3 style="color: #172554; font-size: 19px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">Is cyber insurance required by law?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">
      Cyber liability insurance is not a universal legal requirement for every US business. However, a client, contract, industry requirement or particular business arrangement may require a company to maintain cyber coverage. Privacy and data protection laws can impose obligations even when insurance is not mandatory.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div style="border-bottom-color: rgb(226, 232, 240); border-bottom-style: solid; border-bottom-width: 1px; border-bottom: 1px solid rgb(226, 232, 240); padding: 17px 0px;">
    <h3 style="color: #172554; font-size: 19px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">Can a business buy cyber insurance without cybersecurity measures?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">
      Some insurers may offer coverage to businesses with different levels of cybersecurity maturity, but they can require minimum controls before providing a quote or binding coverage. Security requirements vary by insurer and policy.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div style="border-bottom-color: rgb(226, 232, 240); border-bottom-style: solid; border-bottom-width: 1px; border-bottom: 1px solid rgb(226, 232, 240); padding: 17px 0px;">
    <h3 style="color: #172554; font-size: 19px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">Does general liability insurance cover cyberattacks?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">
      Standard general liability insurance is not designed to provide comprehensive protection against cyber incidents. Cyber-specific coverage may be needed for data breaches, ransomware, privacy claims and cyber-related business interruption. Check the wording of existing policies rather than assuming these risks are included.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div style="border-bottom-color: rgb(226, 232, 240); border-bottom-style: solid; border-bottom-width: 1px; border-bottom: 1px solid rgb(226, 232, 240); padding: 17px 0px;">
    <h3 style="color: #172554; font-size: 19px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">What is the difference between cyber insurance and data breach insurance?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">
      Data breach insurance generally focuses on expenses associated with the exposure of sensitive information. Cyber insurance can be broader, potentially covering ransomware, cyber extortion, business interruption, privacy liability and other covered cyber events.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div style="border-bottom-color: rgb(226, 232, 240); border-bottom-style: solid; border-bottom-width: 1px; border-bottom: 1px solid rgb(226, 232, 240); padding: 17px 0px;">
    <h3 style="color: #172554; font-size: 19px; margin: 0px 0px 8px;">How much cyber insurance coverage does a small business need?</h3>
    <p style="margin: 0px;">
      The appropriate limit depends on the amount and sensitivity of information stored, business revenue, industry, technology dependence, contractual obligations and potential recovery costs. A broker or qualified insurance professional can help assess suitable limits.
    </p>
  </div>

  <h2 style="color: #172554; font-size: 27px; margin-top: 42px;">Final Thoughts: Is Cyber Liability Insurance Right for Your Business?</h2>

  <p>
    Cyber incidents can affect businesses of every size. A data breach, ransomware attack or compromised payment system can create financial losses, disrupt operations and expose a company to legal claims.
  </p>

  <p>
    Cyber liability insurance can help manage some of these costs, including incident investigations, customer notifications, legal defence, data recovery and covered business interruption. However, the protection available depends on the policy's wording, limits, exclusions and conditions.
  </p>

  <p>
    Before purchasing coverage, identify the information your business holds, understand how much you depend on technology and compare the protection offered by different providers. Pay particular attention to first-party and third-party coverage, ransomware conditions, social engineering protection, business interruption and incident response support.
  </p>

  <p>
    Most importantly, do not treat insurance as a replacement for cybersecurity. Maintain strong passwords, enable multifactor authentication, update software, train employees and keep reliable backups. These measures can help reduce your exposure while supporting a stronger overall risk-management strategy.
  </p>

    <h3 style="color: white; font-size: 23px; margin-top: 0px;">Protect Your Business Before an Incident Happens</h3>
    <p style="color: #e2e8f0; margin-bottom: 0px;">
      Review your cyber risks, strengthen your security practices and compare insurance policies carefully. Understanding what is covered before an incident occurs is far better than discovering a coverage gap when you need help.
    </p>
